# Recommendations: Indigenous Principles, Truth and Reconciliation, and Climate Justice in the Two-Hour Course

**Scope.** course.html reviewed against all six curriculum sections, with attention to cultural appropriateness, truth and reconciliation, climate justice, and the Indigenous principles the full curriculum carries. Recommendations are designed to add roughly 700 words total (about 3% of course length, three minutes of reading) and to concentrate changes in the written deep-dive sections rather than the narrated slides, so almost nothing requires re-recording audio.

**Overall assessment.** The course already does several things well: the Module 1 "not monolithic" framing, the relational-crisis framing with Whyte and Cunsolo citations, the marginalization/vulnerability note in Module 3, the cultural safety guidance in Modules 4 and 5, and the four-lens climate justice section. The main gaps are that the course names no Indigenous person, framework, or Nation; it never mentions truth and reconciliation, residential schools, the TRC, or UNDRIP; and Indigenous content appears mostly as perspective-to-respect rather than leadership-in-action. The curriculum contains ready-made, reviewed language for all of these.

---

## A. Core principles to pull over (highest priority)

### 1. Name Etuaptmumk / Two-Eyed Seeing in Module 1
**Where:** Module 1 written section "Indigenous Knowledge Systems and Perspectives," after the Western/Indigenous framing paragraph. Optionally one line on the "Climate change as relational crisis" slide.
**Why:** The slide currently presents "Western frame" and "Indigenous frame" as a two-column contrast. Without a bridging principle this reads as a binary, and comparison tables can imply ranking. Two-Eyed Seeing is the curriculum's guiding principle for exactly this relationship, and it comes with a named Elder and Nation.
**Suggested insertion (~85 words):**
> One widely shared guiding principle for holding these frames together is **Etuaptmumk, or Two-Eyed Seeing**, offered by Mi'kmaw Elder Albert Marshall: "learning to see from one eye with the strengths of Indigenous knowledges and ways of knowing, and from the other eye with the strengths of Western knowledges and ways of knowing, and to using both these eyes together, for the benefit of all." Two-Eyed Seeing avoids ranking knowledge systems and has been used to co-develop climate and mental health programs across Canada.

### 2. Name First Nations, Inuit, and Métis in Module 1
**Where:** Same section, inside the existing "not monolithic" paragraph.
**Why:** The course is Canadian and MHCCA-branded, yet never names the three constitutionally recognized Indigenous Peoples. The curriculum does this in one sentence.
**Suggested insertion (~35 words):**
> In Canada, this includes three constitutionally recognized Indigenous Peoples: First Nations, Inuit, and Métis, each distinct from one another, with unique histories, languages, cultural practices, and relationships with particular lands and waters.

### 3. Add a truth and reconciliation anchor in Module 3
**Where:** Module 3 written section "Population Impacts and Inequities," after the Middleton et al. sentence.
**Why:** The course gestures at "histories of harm" and "colonial dispossession" but never names the specific Canadian history that climate disruption compounds. This is the single largest gap relative to the curriculum, which cites the TRC Final Report, residential schools, and forced relocation directly. One short paragraph carries the idea without a new section.
**Suggested insertion (~95 words):**
> In Canada, this history has a name. Colonial policies, including residential schools, the Sixties Scoop, and forced relocation from traditional territories, are part of why many Indigenous communities are now located in rural, remote, or northern places that face the sharpest climate impacts, and why climate-driven losses of land, water, and traditional foods land on top of intergenerational trauma. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action frame the response: climate-related distress in Indigenous communities cannot be separated from the work of reconciliation, and support for Indigenous healing practices (Call to Action 22) is climate-mental-health work.

### 4. Reframe coping as collective and relational in Module 4
**Where:** Module 4, "Coping Together: Collective and Community-Based Approaches," as a new list item or short paragraph before the climate café list; anchored by the First Nations Mental Wellness Continuum.
**Why:** The coping typology and the collective formats listed (climate cafés, grief circles, World Café) are all Western. The curriculum's most transplantable principle here is that in many Indigenous contexts coping is a collective responsibility, and it names a specific, citable framework with four wellness outcomes.
**Suggested insertion (~90 words):**
> Long before these formats, Indigenous communities structured coping as a shared, relational practice. The **First Nations Mental Wellness Continuum Model** (Thunderbird Partnership Foundation) describes wellness as balance among emotional, physical, mental, and spiritual dimensions, with four outcomes at its centre: hope, belonging, meaning, and purpose. Coping is understood as a collective responsibility rooted in connection to land, ancestors, community, and ceremony: talking and sharing circles, land-based practice, storytelling with Elders, drumming, and beading all support both emotional wellness and cultural continuity. Resilience, in this view, is cultivated through relationship.

### 5. Strengthen the Climate Justice section in Module 5
**Where:** Module 5 written "Climate Justice" section: the Restorative lens card and the closing "In practice" paragraph.
**Why:** Two small edits carry a lot. The course currently lowercases "land back" as a policy instrument; the curriculum treats Land Back as a named, Indigenous-led movement. And the curriculum's strongest justice-to-mental-health insight is missing.
**Suggested edits (~55 words):**
- In the Restorative card, change "land back, reparations, ecological repair" to "**Land Back** (the Indigenous-led movement for restored land governance and self-determination), reparations, ecological repair."
- Add to the "mental health relevance is direct" paragraph: "Recognizing frontline and Indigenous communities as experts, leaders, and protectors of land, rather than as victims of climate change, is itself psychologically stabilizing. This empowerment is not symbolic."

### 6. Ground ecological grief in Inuit experience in Module 2
**Where:** Module 2, "Grief and Solastalgia" panel, first line citing Cunsolo and Ellis.
**Why:** The concept the course cites was developed through research with Inuit communities; crediting that origin grounds the idea in lived Indigenous experience rather than an abstract citation, at the cost of one clause.
**Suggested edit (~20 words):** "...Cunsolo and Ellis (2018), whose concept of ecological grief emerged from research with Inuit communities in Nunatsiavut, Labrador, describe..."

## B. Cultural appropriateness edits (terminology; near-zero length)

### 7. Capitalize "Indigenous Peoples"
The course uses lowercase "peoples" in four places; the curriculum consistently uses "Indigenous Peoples" (about 90 instances), which reflects current Canadian convention and the language of UNDRIP. Find-and-replace, checking each instance.

### 8. Capitalize "Elders" and revise the spiritual-leaders example
The course has three lowercase "elders." Canadian style capitalizes Elders and Knowledge Keepers as titles of respect. Also, Module 5 lists "Indigenous elders leading healing ceremonies" as an example under "Moral and Spiritual Leaders." Elders hold distinct community roles as Knowledge Keepers; presenting them as one example within a clergy-shaped category flattens that. Suggested rewording:
> Elders and Knowledge Keepers leading land-based healing and ceremony in Indigenous-led settings. (Elders hold distinct, sacred roles in their communities; non-Indigenous leaders can support this work rather than emulate it.)

### 9. Enrich the Older Adults lifecourse panel
**Where:** Module 3, "Older Adults" panel, which currently uses "wisdom-keepers" generically.
**Suggested addition (~40 words):**
> In many Indigenous communities this role is formalized: Elders are Knowledge Keepers who support younger generations through storytelling, land-based teaching, and ceremony, while youth are understood as vision holders and future ancestors. Intergenerational climate work draws these roles together.

## C. Let Indigenous voices speak (one or two short quotes)

The course quotes no Indigenous person directly; the curriculum offers several short, sourced quotes. Two would fit without lengthening the audio:

### 10. Kimmerer in Module 4
**Where:** "Land-Based and Indigenous Approaches to Healing" subsection.
> As Robin Wall Kimmerer (Citizen Potawatomi Nation) writes in *Braiding Sweetgrass*: "Knowing that you love the earth changes you, activates you to defend and protect and celebrate. But when you feel that the earth loves you in return, that feeling transforms the relationship from a one-way street into a sacred bond."

### 11. Beverly Jacobs in Module 1 (alternative: Autumn Peltier in Module 3)
**Where:** "Indigenous Knowledge Systems and Perspectives," to make the relational crisis concrete.
> As Beverly Jacobs (Mohawk Nation, Haudenosaunee Confederacy) teaches: "That was our teachings: that you actually see the faces of our children in our land, and if you see the faces of your children in your land, it's alive."

If Module 3 feels light on Indigenous leadership instead, Autumn Peltier (Anishinaabe water-rights advocate, addressing the UN as a teenager) fits the youth lifecourse panel and models climate emotion channelled into action: "We can't eat money or drink oil."

## D. Glossary, objectives, and resources (cheap, high signal)

### 12. Glossary additions (~120 words)
Add entries for: **Etuaptmumk / Two-Eyed Seeing** (Elder Albert Marshall, Mi'kmaw); **Land Back**; **Land-based healing** (health arising from active relationship and stewardship with land, rather than passive access to green space); **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC)** (with one line linking Calls to Action 22 and 92 to climate-mental-health work); **Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK)**.

### 13. Course objectives
"What you'll take away" and the About blurb do not mention Indigenous perspectives or justice, although two of the curriculum's stated objectives are understanding distinct impacts on Indigenous Peoples and the many ways Indigenous Peoples are leading stewardship and climate well-being. Add one line, for example: "An understanding of Indigenous perspectives and climate justice as essential lenses, and of Indigenous leadership in climate and wellness work."

### 14. A "learn more" resource callout
The course already uses MHCCA callout boxes. Add one in Module 1 or 5 mirroring the curriculum's pointers: University of Alberta's *Indigenous Canada* course, SAN'YAS Indigenous-Specific Anti-Racism Training, Yellowhead Institute's Land Back Course, and Climate Atlas of Canada's *Indigenous Knowledges* collection. Near-zero body text; sends motivated learners to Indigenous-authored sources.

## E. Flags and optional items

**15. Assessment alignment.** If items 1 or 3 are adopted, consider swapping one of the 23 final-assessment questions for one on Two-Eyed Seeing or the TRC connection, so the assessment signals that this content is core. Swapping keeps the count at 23.

**16. Land acknowledgment.** The course has none. Whether to add one (and how to make it specific rather than performative) is a judgment call for the team; Native Land Digital, cited in the curriculum, supports personalized acknowledgments. Worth deciding deliberately rather than by default.

**17. Cultural safety review.** The curriculum credits Michael Marchand (sqilxw, syilx Nation) as Cultural Safety Consultant. Any wording adopted from this document, especially items 1, 3, 8, and 16, should go through that review path before publication.

**18. A caution on one statistic.** If the team later imports the curriculum's "Indigenous Peoples safeguard 80% of the world's remaining biodiversity" claim, prefer softer phrasing ("a large share"); the 80% figure has been contested in recent literature.
