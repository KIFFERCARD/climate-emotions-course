Section 2: Climate Emotions – Grief and Solastalgia
Audio Overview — Transcript

As we move from stress and trauma responses into other profound emotional responses triggered by environmental change, it’s important to recognize that not all climate-related distress stems from immediate or anticipated disasters. For many people, the pain runs deeper, anchored in the gradual transformation or outright loss of the places and ecosystems they love. This is where grief and solastalgia emerge as particularly powerful concepts. Grief and solastalgia are also shaped by cultural and relational worldviews, particularly Indigenous perspectives that understand land, identity, and well-being as deeply interconnected and reciprocal.

Grief, often called ecological or climate grief, arises when individuals mourn the degradation of habitats, the disappearance of familiar species, and the slow erosion of ways of life tied to the natural world. Farmers who once relied on predictable rainfall might feel a deep sense of sorrow as drought becomes the new norm, and Indigenous communities may grieve the desecration of lands that have sustained them for generations.

Solastalgia, coined by Australian philosopher Glenn Albrecht, takes this sense of loss a step further, describing the homesickness people feel when the environment around them changes so drastically that it no longer feels like home. It’s a kind of mourning that happens while you’re still physically present in the place you cherish—an unsettling disconnect as beaches recede under rising tides or with more subtle changes such as the gradual change of a boreal forest shifting to a different mix of trees. Both ecological grief and solastalgia can be profoundly disruptive, weighing heavily on mental well-being. Symptoms may include persistent sadness, a sense of alienation, or even anger directed at perceived causes of the environmental damage.

In some cases, when processed in a supportive environment, these emotions can be powerful drivers of growth and constructive change, such as through post-traumatic growth (PTG). By acknowledging these feelings as valid and deeply human responses to a changing planet, communities and individuals can create supportive networks, find ways to reconnect with nature, and develop coping strategies that honor their grief without succumbing to hopelessness. These emotions can also drive collective action, fueling efforts to protect and restore at-risk ecosystems, preserve cultural traditions, and push for sustainable policies.

Ultimately, recognizing the emotional toll of ecological change offers a path toward healing and resilience, ensuring that people—and the places they hold dear—are not left behind in confronting climate challenges.
