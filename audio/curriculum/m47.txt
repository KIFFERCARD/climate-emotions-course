Section 5: Professional Roles - Health and Social Care Providers
Audio Overview — Transcript

When heatwaves send older adults to the emergency room, or floods disrupt entire communities, it is often nurses, social workers, and community health providers who first witness the anxieties, grief, and trauma that follow. Their positions, grounded in ethical frameworks like the Hippocratic Oath and public health principles, empower them to address not only the physical symptoms of climate impacts but also the deeper emotional wounds that can linger long after a disaster subsides.

This responsibility aligns naturally with core medical ethics, which mandate “do no harm” and stress the importance of beneficence, justice, and autonomy. Recognizing that climate threats disproportionately affect low-income communities, Indigenous populations, and those with chronic illnesses, health professionals become frontline defenders against new forms of vulnerability. They have the influence to advocate for more resilient infrastructure—such as designing clinics that can endure extreme weather—and to integrate mental health support into everything from routine check-ups to emergency response plans. In this way, their role extends beyond one-on-one patient care, reaching into public health policy and community engagement.

For many, one of the most pressing concerns is the spike in mental health needs tied to environmental upheaval. Eco-anxiety, climate grief, and stress linked to displacement are increasingly common, yet still relatively under-recognized. By acknowledging these issues as legitimate responses rather than overreactions, health and social care providers can create safe spaces for individuals to express their fears or sadness. Instead of pathologizing these emotions, they can employ approaches like trauma-informed care, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, or group-based interventions where communities process climate distress together. This support might include teaching coping skills for managing heatwaves, preparing families for hurricane season, or simply offering psychoeducation on the links between climate change and mental health.

Addressing social determinants of health is equally crucial. Factors like inadequate housing, limited access to clean water, and unstable employment can multiply the risks posed by climate shifts. Here, social workers and community health workers have a unique vantage point, as they often develop close relationships with marginalized groups and see firsthand how environmental problems add layers of stress to already precarious situations. By working with local organizations, these professionals can promote culturally sensitive strategies, ranging from organizing cooling centers during a heatwave to advocating for policies that reduce pollution in disadvantaged neighborhoods. Such efforts mirror the World Health Organization’s emphasis on climate-resilient health systems, which call for comprehensive approaches that look beyond the clinic walls.

Building on this, culturally safe and equitable care also recognizes the importance of Indigenous traditional and healing practices within health care systems. Health and Social care providers who work in partnership with Elders, Knowledge Keepers, and cultural practitioners can help bridge Western clinical services with Indigenous healing modalities, ensuring care is not only accessible, but affirming, and grounded in relationships that extend beyond the individual to land, family, and community

One reason health and social care providers stand out in a whole-of-society approach is their trusted status. People generally trust doctors, nurses, and social workers to offer unbiased, evidence-based guidance. This trust can be channeled to influence both individual behaviors—like encouraging individuals and families to adopt environmentally friendly practices—and systemic change, such as lobbying for better air quality regulations. Hospitals and health networks can also lead by example, reducing their own carbon footprints and demonstrating what responsible environmental stewardship looks like in practice.

Ultimately, professionals in these fields bridge the gap between immediate relief and long-term resilience. In the aftermath of a disaster, providing trauma support and rebuilding social connections lays the groundwork for healing. Over time, advocating for improved public health measures, pushing for equitable resource allocation, and educating communities about the mental and physical toll of climate change ensures that people are better prepared for whatever comes next. This blend of direct care, policy engagement, and community collaboration underscores why health and social care professionals are indispensable partners in the wider movement to address climate challenges. Through their dedication to safeguarding well-being—even in the most vulnerable corners of society—they help forge a path toward healthier, more resilient communities in a rapidly changing world.
