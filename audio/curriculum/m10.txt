Section 2: Theories of Emotion
Audio Overview — Transcript

To better understand emotions, one starting point is to think about the broad theories that shape common understandings of what emotions are. In this module, we explore some of these theories, making note of key concepts that shape current research and understandings of climate and mental health. The focus is primarily on Western research and theory related to emotions as it provides a basis for most academic research and emerging approaches to climate emotions. However, it is important to acknowledge that this represents only one lens among many and this section is not exhaustive. Across the world, Indigenous and non-Western communities possess unique knowledges, frameworks, and ways of understanding emotions, including how they arise and how they connect with the environment and well-being. For example, Indigenous scholarship such as Dian Million’s Felt theory emphasizes Indigenous women’s emotions and lived experiences as legitimate forms of knowledge shaped by colonial histories, while Michael Yellow Bird’s work on neurodecolonization highlights how colonization-related stress can affect the brain, emotions, and well-being.

The theories to be discussed further include evolutionary, physiological, cognitive, neuroscientific, and social or cultural explanations. Each of these five categories helps explain a different piece of the emotional puzzle. Evolutionary theories show how emotions enhance survival and communication, physiological theories explain how emotions stem from bodily responses. From a cognitive theory perspective, emotions depend on how we interpret and label experiences, while neuroscientific theories are rooted in specific brain regions and circuits. Social and cultural theories explain how emotions are shaped by culture, context and interaction. Together they show how biology, interpretation, survival instincts, brain processes, and cultural norms all intertwine in the face of stressors like climate change.

In the following segment, we will introduce each of these types of explanations and their origins, and then follow these introductions with an overview of more current and integrative approaches to the science of emotions.

To begin, evolutionary theories claim that emotions evolved to help humans survive and communicate, an idea famously put forth by Charles Darwin in the 1870s. This perspective suggests that emotions like fear guide us away from danger, while emotions like love promote bonding. Later, in the 1980s, Robert Plutchik built on this notion by proposing eight primary emotions—such as joy, fear, sadness, and anger—that can mix into more complex emotional states, all of which he believed supported essential survival needs.

Physiological theories put the body front and center, suggesting that emotional feelings come from our awareness of bodily changes. Within this category, the James-Lange Theory, proposed in the late 1800s, argues that we feel afraid because we recognize our heart pounding or our muscles tensing, making the bodily reaction the spark that creates the emotion. This view was later challenged in the 1920s by the Cannon-Bard Theory, which contended that bodily responses and the experience of emotion happen together but independently, so a racing heartbeat and a feeling of fear can occur at the same moment rather than in a strict sequence. A further development, the Schachter-Singer Two-Factor Theory of the 1960s, introduced the idea that we need both a surge of physiological arousal and a cognitive label to form an emotion, meaning that the same pounding heart could be interpreted as excitement or fear depending on context. Additionally, The Facial Feedback Hypothesis, which also emerged later, but traces back to Charles Darwin and William James’ work suggests that forming a specific facial expression can influence the emotion you feel.

Meanwhile, cognitive theories focus on how our thoughts, interpretations, and appraisals frame emotional experiences. Richard Lazarus, for instance, argued in the 1980s that we evaluate or “appraise” a situation’s impact on our goals or well-being, and this evaluation triggers an emotional response. In other words, if we believe someone disrespected us for instance, we feel anger because of the meaning we assign to that event. Then we have neuroscientific theories, where researchers explore how the brain itself processes emotion. Joseph LeDoux introduced the idea of two distinct neural pathways in the 1990s: a “low road,” which triggers rapid, unconscious emotional reactions, and a “high road,” which involves more deliberate, conscious thought before we feel the full emotion. Around the same time, Jaak Panksepp’s affective neuroscience work traced emotions to ancient brain circuits, identifying core emotional systems like seeking, rage, and care that shape our most fundamental responses.

Lastly, social and cultural theories remind us that where and how we live can have a profound impact on our emotions. A social constructionist perspective emphasizes that cultural values and social norms shape which emotions we consider appropriate and how we express them, so an event that triggers pride in one culture might provoke shame in another. Even so, research by Paul Ekman in the 1970s found that certain basic emotions—happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise—are recognized across many cultures, while cultural “display rules” still influence how openly these feelings are shown. Lev Vygotsky’s sociocultural theory likewise underscores that emotional development is grounded in social interactions and shaped by language and cultural practices.

More current emotion science blends all these viewpoints into a multidimensional perspective that acknowledges the biological, cognitive, and social forces creating our emotional world. For example, Lisa Feldman Barrett’s Constructed Theory of Emotion, introduced in the 2000s, challenges the notion of universal emotions by arguing that our brains create emotions using a combination of bodily sensations, memories, and social cues, leading to wide variability in how we label our feelings.

Building on this broader perspective, recent studies on climate change and emotions, highlighted within the module, also show that emotional responses to climate change vary globally, with patterns of fear, hope, anger and sadness differing across countries and being shaped by exposure to climate impacts, cultural contexts, and structural inequalities.

By recognizing the roles of our bodies, our survival instincts, our neural wiring, our thought processes, and our cultural settings in recognizing and experiencing emotions, we can develop more effective ways to cope with the uncertainties of environmental shifts, support one another’s mental well-being, and build resilience in our communities.
