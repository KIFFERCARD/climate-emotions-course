Section 5: Professional Roles - Educators
Audio Overview — Transcript

Educators—from preschool teachers to university professors—often find themselves on the front line of addressing climate anxieties and the emotional toll these challenges can bring. It’s no longer enough to share facts about melting ice caps or greenhouse gases; students are grappling with issues of grief, worry, and a deep sense of uncertainty about the future. By recognizing the power they hold to shape not only what students know but also how they feel and act, educators become pivotal agents in building emotional resilience and collective hope.

In many ways, teachers and professors serve as a bridge between evidence-based science and the human experience. It starts with climate literacy, ensuring that students understand what’s happening to the planet, how our actions contribute to those changes, and what solutions are possible. Yet climate literacy on its own can leave students feeling overwhelmed if their emotional responses are ignored. That’s why so many educational frameworks now emphasize social-emotional learning (SEL) alongside environmental content, giving learners the vocabulary and coping strategies to name and navigate fears or grief. In essence, classrooms become spaces where students can process heavy emotions, rather than suppress them or face them alone.

This approach is rooted in theories like Transformative Learning, which invites learners to question their own assumptions and envision new possibilities, and Critical Pedagogy, which challenges them to see climate change through a lens of social justice and systemic inequities. If a child comes to class distressed about rising sea levels, for instance, the educator’s role is not just to validate those feelings but to encourage problem-solving, agency, and forward-thinking. That might look like analyzing real-world data on coastal erosion, organizing a school-based sustainability campaign, or even partnering with local community groups to address environmental concerns. By weaving in elements of Ecopsychology and Positive Psychology, educators help students channel their emotional responses into hope and collective action, rather than despair.

One compelling way to do this is through experiential learning—taking students outside to restore a wetland or plant trees, where they can see tangible evidence of both environmental harm and renewal. Activities like these foster place-based connections and remind learners that they are part of a larger ecological community. Meanwhile, educators can also introduce culturally responsive practices that highlight how disproportionately impacted populations, such as Indigenous Peoples and communities in the Global South experience and respond to environmental impacts in distinct ways. In doing so, the classroom becomes a microcosm of the broader world, showing students that climate solutions must be equitable and inclusive to be truly sustainable.

Educators exploring the intersection of climate change and mental health can also draw inspiration from Indigenous models of teaching that center relationships, place, and community. In many Indigenous worldviews, the land is not just a setting for learning, but a teacher itself. Land-based education connects learners to cycles, kinship, responsibility, and the emotional grounding needed to face environmental change. Within these approaches, storytelling and the wisdom of Elders are also central to Indigenous climate education. Stories do more than convey information. They teach how to live well with each other and with the land.

Drawing from these approaches, educators then, are more than just conveyors of information; they are mentors who help learners unpack emotions like eco-anxiety, anger, or even numbness. They model how to discuss difficult topics honestly and respectfully, and they cultivate the sense that when people come together—in class debates, group projects, or local volunteer efforts—they can make a meaningful difference. These experiences can shift a student’s perspective from one of helplessness to one of agency, where climate change is understood not only as a global emergency but also as an invitation to learn, grow, and enact positive change.

Across different levels—whether in a primary school setting or a graduate seminar—an educator’s influence extends far beyond the walls of a classroom. It ripples into many different areas, where tomorrow’s leaders will apply the knowledge and emotional resilience they’ve gained. By bringing climate science, mental health awareness, and a commitment to collective well-being into their work, educators can galvanize a generation that not only understands the magnitude of the problem but also believes in—and works for—real solutions. In that sense, their role becomes indispensable to a whole-of-society response, ensuring that as the world changes, our capacity for empathy, solidarity, and innovation grows with it.
