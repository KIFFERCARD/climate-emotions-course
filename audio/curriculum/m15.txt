Section 2: Climate Emotions - Stress and Trauma Responses
Audio Overview — Transcript

In some instances, climate distress can go beyond the general worry or anxiety and extend into stress or trauma responses. These can occur in anticipation of potential climate-related disasters, in the aftermath of experiencing climate-related crises, or when witnessing others being affected by the impacts of climate change.

Anticipatory stress and trauma responses can be marked by persistent dread and an unshakable sense of looming catastrophe. People may struggle to focus at work or school because they’re constantly imagining the devastation of a future flood, hurricane, or drought, and this worry becomes more than just a fleeting concern—it shapes their day-to-day thinking, sleep patterns, and ability to relax or plan for the future. Even before a disaster occurs, they might experience physical symptoms like headaches, muscle tension, or digestive issues, as their bodies remain in a near-constant state of stress. Over time, if the feared events do happen or media coverage intensifies, these reactions can intensify and lead to more entrenched mental health challenges.

Stress or trauma responses can also arise after a person has lived through or witnessed climate-driven crises such as wildfires ravaging entire communities, hurricanes destroying neighbourhoods, or repeated floods forcing families to relocate. In these cases, individuals may develop symptoms that seriously disrupt their lives. Nightmares and flashbacks can replay terrifying moments, making it hard to get restful sleep or even feel safe during the day. Hypervigilance—an overactive sense of alertness—can make everyday noises or weather updates trigger spikes of panic. Emotional numbness, depression, and detachment from friends and family can also set in, while avoidance behaviors lead some people to steer clear of anything that reminds them of the disaster (like certain news channels or weather reports). These symptoms can persist long after the event, affecting relationships, job performance, and overall well-being.

These responses can also occur indirectly through secondary traumatic stress (STS) and vicarious trauma, where individuals are impacted by exposure to others’ traumatic experiences. Secondary trauma can arise suddenly and present with PTSD-like symptoms, whereas vicarious trauma often develops over time and can impact a person’s cognitive beliefs.  This is particularly relevant for professionals such as healthcare workers, emergency responders, and journalists who may be exposed through their work.

When repeated extreme weather hits the same region, cumulative trauma can multiply these effects, leaving entire communities caught in a cycle of apprehension before disasters and psychological upheaval afterward. These trauma responses have an emotional toll more profound than typical worry or sadness: they reshape how people see themselves, their environment, and their future, underscoring the urgent need for targeted mental health support in climate adaptation and disaster recovery efforts. For example, the community of Kashechewan First Nation which has experienced repeated seasonal flooding and evacuations, can create ongoing stress, particularly when climate impacts intersect with longer histories of colonialism and displacement. This community has also worked hard to improve their adaptive capacity and emergency response, highlighting the community’s resilience to ongoing stressors.

Addressing stress and trauma responses may involve a combination of individual support (such as CBT and somatic approaches), community-based approaches (such as climate cafes and peer support groups), and system-level interventions that ensure access to timely and culturally safe mental health care. Additionally, the use of a trauma-informed approach is essential to responding to climate-related stress and trauma at all levels.
