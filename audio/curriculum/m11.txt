Section 2: Categorizing Emotions
Audio Overview — Transcript

Now that we’ve explored how different theories explain where emotions come from, let’s turn to some of the ways these emotions can be classified. This supports our broader look at mental health and climate change because the categories and dimensions of our emotional experiences can affect how we cope with environmental challenges.

There are several different approaches to categorizing emotions. Building on some of the theories presented in the last module, Charles Darwin’s evolutionary perspective influenced later researchers such as Paul Ekman and Carol Izard, who suggested that some emotions can be understood as “basic” or discrete emotions. These are thought to be innate, universal feelings that arise quickly and do not require complex thought. Examples include happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise. However, as discussed in the last module, the theory of universal emotions has more recently been challenged and emotional responses are more likely to combine a range of social, cultural, biological, physiological, and other factors.

Another related approach comes from Robert Plutchik, who conceptualized and arranged eight primary emotions in a wheel, suggesting that they can combine (like trust and joy forming love), vary in intensity, and even stand in opposition to one another. Other approaches, like Parrott’s model, group emotions into families or hierarchies, showing how broader emotions can branch into specific feelings. In contrast, dimensional models such as Russell’s Circumplex Model and the PAD model shift away from fixed categories and map emotions along continuous dimensions such as valence, arousal, and dominance. These models emphasize that emotions exist on spectrums rather than in fixed categories.

More recent hybrid and integrative models, including MIT’s affective learning models and HUMAINE’s EARL framework, combine categorical and dimensional approaches to better capture complexity of emotional experiences. Cognitive appraisal theories, such as the OCC model emphasize that emotions arise from how we evaluate events, agents and situations. Cultural-lexical approaches, like The Book of Human Emotions highlight how emotion language is shaped by culture, history, and language.

Indigenous perspectives further broaden this understanding by situating emotions within relationships to land, community, spirit and overall well-being as reflected in holistic models such as the First Nations perspective on Health and Wellness and Social and Emotional Wellbeing frameworks.

Modern views often mix these ideas, blending insights from different approaches. All of this helps us see that emotions are anything but one-size-fits-all: we may share certain common reactions, yet our experiences can still vary widely based on how we interpret our world, the cultural rules we’ve learned, and the unique stresses—such as those related to climate change—that shape our lives. By grasping these classification systems, we can gain tools to help understand why someone might respond to an environmental crisis with panic, while someone else feels determined or even hopeful, and how interventions can be tailored to address the specific emotional patterns within different communities.
