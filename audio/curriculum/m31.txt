Differentiating Between More and Less Adaptive Responses
Audio Overview — Transcript

Imagine you’re standing at the crossroads of different paths. On one path, you acknowledge the serious reality of climate change and you feel anxious or sad about it, but those emotions spur you to reach out, learn more, and find ways to engage with others who are taking action. On another path, you either try to block out the problem of climate change or sink into a sense of doom so heavy that it feels impossible to do anything at all. These paths capture different ways people respond to the ecological crisis. In reality, people often move between these responses or “paths” or experience a combination depending on the situation, resources, and support available at different times.

Adaptive and maladaptive, or more and less adaptive cognitions, refer to the different ways people think about and respond to climate-related stress. More adaptive cognitions tend to support constructive problem-solving, acceptance, and action, while less adaptive cognitions may temporarily reduce distress, but can lead to denial, distortion, or rumination that interferes with effective coping.

Over the last few decades, psychologists have drawn on a variety of theories and intellectual traditions to understand why some people respond to climate change in ways that preserve their well-being and foster meaningful action, while others may become overwhelmed, immobilized or engage in a way that can be harmful to well-being.

From a stress and coping perspective, pioneered by Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman, individuals constantly evaluate environmental demands—such as rising temperatures, disasters, or ecological uncertainty—and assess whether they have the resources to cope. When climate threats are appraised as manageable, this supports adaptive coping, including problem-solving and acceptance. When they are seen as overwhelming, maladaptive coping such as denial or rumination may emerge, limiting effective action and increasing distress.

Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), associated with Aaron Beck and Albert Ellis, highlights how maladaptive thought patterns—such as catastrophizing or all-or-nothing thinking—can intensify distress and reduce effective coping. Adaptive thinking involves balanced, realistic perspectives that acknowledge climate risks while supporting practical, incremental action in daily life, community involvement, or policy engagement.

Emotion regulation theory, particularly James Gross’s work, focuses on how people manage emotional responses to climate change. Adaptive regulation often involves reappraisal, where emotions like anxiety are reframed in ways that support coping and action. Maladaptive regulation, such as suppression or avoidance, may reduce distress in the short term but can contribute to burnout, disengagement, or emotional exhaustion over time.

Existential perspectives, including the work of Viktor Frankl and Irvin Yalom, emphasize how climate change can evoke questions of meaning, mortality, and purpose. Ecopsychologists such as Theodore Roszak and Joanna Macy highlight how disconnection from nature can intensify distress, while reconnection can support well-being. More adaptive responses involve finding meaning amid uncertainty, such as aligning actions with values, spending time in nature, or engaging in community care. Less adaptive responses may involve nihilism or withdrawal, where individuals feel that their actions no longer matter.

Social Constructionism developed by Peter L. Berger and Thomas Luckmann, and Critical Psychology highlight how climate emotions are shaped by cultural narratives, norms, and systems of power. These frameworks emphasize that responses to climate change are embedded in broader sociopolitical contexts, not only individual psychology.

Positive psychology and hope theory, associated with C. R. Snyder, Martin Seligman, and Barbara Fredrickson, emphasize hope as an active cognitive process rather than simple optimism. Adaptive hope involves recognizing climate challenges while maintaining a sense of agency and capacity to contribute to solutions. This supports persistence and engagement even when progress is slow. Less adaptive forms of hope may appear as unrealistic optimism or “toxic positivity,” where risks are minimized and difficult emotions are dismissed.

Beyond psychological theories, Indigenous wellness frameworks, such as the First Nations Wellness Continuum Model, highlight that well-being is strengthened through relationships, culture, community,  land, and supportive systems emphasizing that adaptation can be both an individual and collective process.

Throughout these varied perspectives, the thread remains consistent: more adaptive responses can help us come to terms with reality, remain emotionally grounded, and maintain a sense of efficacy and hope in the face of daunting challenges. Less adaptive responses often lead us either to deny the problem or become constantly overwhelmed by it, eroding the determination and creativity we need. Recognizing the difference is a key step toward building resilience, both for ourselves and for our communities. By understanding how our thoughts and feelings can either propel us forward or hold us back, we gain the power to shape our emotional responses in ways that foster constructive action and long-term well-being.
