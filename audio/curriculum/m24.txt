Welcome to Section 3: Factors Influencing Mental Health in a Changing Climate!

When we think about climate emotions, whether it’s anxiety, grief, anger, or hope, it’s helpful to understand that these feelings don’t arise in a vacuum. Emotions are functional; they’re part of how we adapt, survive, and make sense of the world around us. In the context of climate change, these emotions are responses to very real challenges: rising sea levels, extreme weather events, environmental degradation, political inaction and the deep social inequalities that climate change exposes. They’re not just random reactions; they’re signals telling us something important about the world and our place in it.

In the modules ahead, we’ll unpack these layers in more detail. First, we’ll explore the social and environmental drivers of climate emotions, looking at how the climate crisis itself—rising temperatures, biodiversity loss, extreme weather—creates emotional responses. We’ll also examine how social factors, like culture, political debates or media narratives, amplify or mitigate these feelings, and consider the ways in which these social and environmental drivers overlap and interact. Then, we’ll dive into developmental and lifespan perspectives, exploring how age and life stage shape how people perceive and respond to climate threats. From there, we’ll turn to marginalization and vulnerability, considering how some communities—whether due to economic, geographic, political, systemic, or cultural factors—are more exposed to both environmental risks and their emotional tolls. This will lead us into a discussion of intersectionality, which explores how overlapping identities such as race, gender, socioeconomic status, age and disability create unique experiences of climate emotions.

By the end of this section, you’ll have a deeper understanding of why people feel the way they do about climate change—not just in terms of individual reactions, but also through the broader social, environmental, and systemic contexts that shape these emotions. Together, this knowledge can help us move beyond despair and towards solutions that are compassionate, inclusive, and just. Let’s get started.
