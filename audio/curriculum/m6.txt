Section 2: Indigenous Knowledge Systems, Frameworks, and Climate-Mental Health Connections
Audio Overview — Transcript

Indigenous Peoples, through their remaining traditional lands and territories, are stewards to a remarkable portion of the world’s biodiversity – often cited around 80% of what remains – and they do so while living in deep relationship with the lands they steward. Indigenous worldviews weave together ecological, spiritual, social, and mental well-being into one continuum. In discussions about climate change and mental health, this perspective is incredibly important. Yet it is also important to remember that Indigenous communities are on the frontlines of climate impacts and carry the weight of colonization’s legacy as well as ongoing colonization. Understanding and centering Indigenous knowledge systems is not just an added bonus; it’s indispensable to any climate policy or practice that aims to address climate-related distress and build collective resilience.

To honour Indigenous Peoples perspectives this audio section will contain more information than other audio sections as the deep knowledge of what Indigenous Peoples experience as well as their knowledge and wisdom is often held in oral language and in the relational nature of listening to and hearing the voices of others. Additionally this section also contains a brief overview of some of the more common impacts and experiences of historical and ongoing colonization for distinct and diverse Indigenous Peoples.

Many Indigenous Peoples have experienced cultural, community and familial dislocation and loss of access to their ancestral lands. To fully grasp the mental health impacts of climate change for Indigenous Peoples, we need to look at the historical and structural context. Climate hardships today are woven into a larger fabric of historical and ongoing colonialism, violence, and displacement. And these aren’t just background facts; they actively shape how communities can respond to climate threats. If a community was forced off their fertile land onto a flood-prone reserve, or if their families were torn apart by government policies in the last century, their ability to cope with a wildfire or a heatwave today is affected by those past and ongoing traumas.

Historic colonization and violence against Indigenous Peoples – through forced removals, residential boarding schools, and events like the Sixties Scoop – fractured family systems and severed the connection to land. These policies tried to break Indigenous identity and resulted in deep intergenerational trauma. That trauma can amplify climate distress today. For example, losing a piece of land to erosion isn’t just losing property; it’s reopening a wound of an earlier loss of lands taken.

There was also systemic cultural suppression: for a long time, Indigenous ceremonies and languages were banned or discouraged by law. These were attempts to erase Indigenous ways of knowing. Yet, communities found ways to preserve their knowledge – sometimes in secret, sometimes through open resistance and revival movements. On top of that, displacement and environmental racism played a role. Many Indigenous communities were pushed to less desirable lands or saw mining and pipelines cut through their territories without consent. These practices increased their exposure to environmental hazards and stripped away resources that could help in adapting to climate change.

Despite these assaults, Indigenous Nations have shown extraordinary resilience. They have continuously regenerated their governance systems, languages, and healing practices. Many communities are reviving traditional farming and aquaculture, restoring pre-colonial place names, and reasserting legal rights to their land. These aren’t just symboli cultural projects – they’re life-saving and ecologically important. They offer time-tested models of resilience and a sense of identity and strength that the broader climate-mental-health field urgently needs to learn from. In other words, while colonial policies and legacies create vulnerabilities, Indigenous strengths and renewal efforts provide powerful tools for healing and climate and community adaptation.

It’s also important to recognize that Indigenous knowledge is not monolithic. There are hundreds of Distinct Nations in BC alone, each with its own languages, teachings, and practices. An Anishinaabe medicine story is not the same as a Māori ocean stewardship practice, for example. However, across these diverse traditions, there exist common threads. Indigenous intellectual traditions emphasize relationality and reciprocity – meaning everything is connected, and knowledge comes from relationships with the land, water, animals, and community. Knowledge isn’t just in books; it’s on the land and passed down through living experience. Elders, land guardians, and Indigenous scholars are the knowledge holders who carry these teachings. They transmit and adapt wisdom through storytelling, oral history, ceremony, and daily practice. Examining a few key thinkers and traditions that have been especially influential in reframing climate and mental health discussions is an important starting point.

Despite growing recognition that Indigenous knowledge systems hold valuable lessons, there are still common challenges that prevent these systems from being fully and fairly integrated into climate responses. A major issue is epistemic injustice, which means not all knowledge is treated as equal. Indigenous experts might be invited to conferences or policy discussions, but too often it’s done in a tokenistic way or their knowledge and scholarship is not seen as having mainstream academic applications. For example: an Indigenous scholar might get to speak or provide a ceremonial opening, yet when it comes to shaping the actual policy or research design, their knowledge isn’t given real authority. This kind of tokenism and dismissal can be harmful because it creates an illusion of inclusion while perpetuating the status quo.

Another challenge is the fragmentation or policy silos in governments and institutions. There often exist separate departments or funds for mental health, for the environment, for Indigenous affairs, and so on. Each works in its own bubble. But Indigenous approaches are often holistic – they don’t separate mental well-being from land or culture. So if a community wants to launch a program that combines ecological restoration with youth counseling and cultural teachings, they might have trouble funding it because no single silo fully supports that holistic approach. Western or mainstream systems aren’t set up to easily handle solutions that cut across categories, which are exactly the kinds of solutions Indigenous frameworks often propose.

There’s also the issue of knowledge appropriation. This happens when elements of Indigenous cultures (say, the concept of the Medicine Wheel or a particular ceremony or story) are taken out of context and used by non-Indigenous organizations without permission or understanding. For example, a wellness retreat might borrow a smudging ceremony because it “seems healing” but do it in a way that’s divorced from its cultural protocols. Not only can that offend and hurt the community it’s taken from, it also usually means the practice loses its effectiveness – because the power of these knowledge systems lies in the relationships and context around them. Appropriating knowledge without proper respect, credit, or community control erodes trust and can even do harm to the very people an intervention is supposed to help.

So, what can be done? How can siloed institutions and organizations move from just acknowledging Indigenous knowledge to integrating it equitably and effectively in climate and mental health work? There are several strategies emerging, and they all require a shift in power and approach.

A first step is supporting, Indigenous-led governance. This means putting Indigenous leadership at the center of decision-making, not just on the sidelines. For example, rather than a government agency designing a climate adaptation program for an Indigenous community, the agency would support that community in designing and governing the program themselves. Some climate funds are starting to do this by reserving a portion of funding that is controlled by Indigenous boards, who decide what projects to prioritize. The idea is that Indigenous communities know best what their people and lands need, so they should have authority, not just a consulting role.

Second, ethical knowledge co-production. Two-Eyed Seeing can be a guiding principle for this. In practice, it means researchers and Indigenous knowledge holders working together from day one on a project. They set goals together, decide how data will be owned and shared (honoring Indigenous data sovereignty, which asserts that communities have control over the data collected from their lands and peoples), and make sure the methods respect cultural protocols. An example might be a mental health study where Western psychologists and Indigenous elders design the survey together – including questions that make sense in the community’s worldview – and they agree that the community will keep the data after the study, or have a say in how the results are used. When done right, this kind of co-production builds trust and yields solutions that are effective because they resonate with people’s lived experiences.

Another key strategy is supporting Indigenous land rights and stewardship – often encapsulated in the phrase “Land Back.” This doesn’t always literally mean giving all land back immediately (though in some cases it can involve returning specific parcels or co-managing them). It’s about legally recognizing that Indigenous communities have legislative and legal rights and responsibility to steward their traditional territories. Why mention this in a climate-mental-health context? Because land is medicine. When land is returned or co-managed and allowed to heal, people heal too. One can see this in places where, say, a river is returned to Indigenous stewardship – the water quality improves, the fish come back, and along with that, the community’s sense of hope and purpose rises. People, especially the youth, feel proud seeing their Nation lead environmental restoration. It combats the despair of climate change with real, tangible action and a restored connection to place that extends outward to the broader community that gains the benefits of greater biodiversity and ecological resilience as well as the benefits of renaturing provide to everyone.

In designing health and social services, there’s a push for trauma-informed and strength-based  approaches that blend Indigenous and Western practices. For example, consider a mental health program in a First Nations community: an assessment might use something akin to the Medicine Wheel framework to understand a person’s well-being in a holistic way – looking at spiritual, emotional, physical, and mental health together. The care plan that follows could include sessions with a Western-trained counselor and time on the land with a knowledge keeper learning to harvest medicines, and perhaps a family ceremony. Rather than assuming Western therapy alone is the fix, these combined approaches acknowledge that culture is a form of healing. And they recognize the strength and wisdom already present in the community. Moreover, where Western clinical care is used, it’s done in a way that’s culturally safer and adapted to fit, not one-size-fits-all.

A big piece of the puzzle is sustainable funding and support. It’s one thing to start a great initiative – say, a land-based youth camp where young people learn hunting, fishing, or gardening from Elders as a means to build resilience and life skills. It’s another to keep it running year after year. Many of these programs rely on short-term grants. A holistic program might not show flashy results in six months on a spreadsheet, but over years, it can be transformative – reducing youth suicide rates, improving school retention, and healing relationships. For instance, there have been On-the-Land programs in northern communities that report declines in depression and even suicidality among youth who participate. These successes tell us that we need to commit to long-term funding, treating these programs as essential infrastructure for climate adaptation, not just temporary projects. Investing in Indigenous-led, land-based and cultural programs is an investment in community resilience that pays off in lives saved and futures brightened.

Now, let’s bring this back to the heart of our module: climate emotions and mental health. For many Indigenous Peoples, the climate crisis isn’t abstract or distant – it’s very intimate and emotional, because it threatens places and relatives (including non-human relatives like animals and plants) that they deeply love. The term eco-anxiety takes on a different shade here; it might be the anxiety of an Inuit hunter who sees the sea ice thinning and worries not just for his livelihood, but for the survival of his culture that depends on the ice. Ecological grief might be the grief of an Elder mourning the death of an ancient cedar tree that was like a family member. These emotions run deep, and they’re often layered on top of historical trauma.

However, Indigenous frameworks offer unique strengths for addressing these climate-related emotions in ways that are culturally safe and transformative. One of those strengths is a sense of agency and belonging through land-based action. When people feel overwhelmed by climate change, one of the most powerful antidotes is to do something meaningful. Indigenous communities have long practiced what we now call “nature-based solutions” – restoring wetlands, conducting controlled burns, replanting native species – not only for physical survival but as a way of life that reinforces identity. Such land-based actions can turn helplessness into purpose. If a young person is anxious about a changing climate, joining their community in a river cleanup or a traditional fishing camp can give them a direct sense of contribution. They see the fish returning or the forest regrowing and think, “I am part of this positive change.” That feeling of purpose and belonging on the land can counteract despair.

Another strength is collective coping through storytelling and ceremony. In many Indigenous cultures, when there’s a loss – say an ancestral territory is damaged – the response isn’t just private sadness; it’s communal grieving. Storytelling, community gatherings, healing dances, seasonal ceremonies – these are ways people come together to honor what was lost, to share grief openly, and to support one another. It transforms individual grief into shared resilience. For example, after a devastating wildfire, a community might hold a ceremony to mourn the old forest and to commit to protecting the land as it regenerates. In that circle, tears might be shed, but so are prayers and promises. This collective process means people aren’t left alone with their anxiety or sadness; it’s held by the group and often directed into hope and action.

Intergenerational healing is another critical piece. Climate change is inherently intergenerational – its causes and effects span multiple generations. Indigenous knowledge emphasizes the responsibility to future generations, but also the healing of past generational trauma. Revitalizing language, songs, and practices that were once banned has a profound healing effect in communities. It repairs the cultural fabric that colonialism tried to tear apart. When the cultural fabric is stronger, the whole community’s mental health improves – kind of like raising the baseline of well-being. So when climate-related stresses hit, whether it’s an acute disaster or a slow-moving change like permafrost melt, communities that have been investing in cultural strength are better able to withstand the emotional strain. A young person who knows their language and traditional teachings may face a disaster with the knowledge, “We’ve gone through hard times and we have teachings on how to cope and rebuild,” rather than feeling completely lost.

From a policy and program perspective, including Indigenous frameworks makes interventions more effective for everyone, not just Indigenous Peoples. These frameworks tend to be holistic and sustainable. For instance, a conservation project designed with Indigenous input might also include youth education, mental health supports, and economic development, all wrapped together. That means it’s tackling multiple issues at once – climate, education, health, livelihoods – which ultimately leads to stronger buy-in and longer-term success. Non-Indigenous communities can benefit from these approaches too, because they address the root causes and the human element, not just the environmental symptom.  Examples of community forestry initiatives or disaster response plans that, after incorporating Indigenous knowledge (like where to build safely, how to manage water, etc.), end up saving money and lives when crises hit demonstrate the importance of collaboration and reconciliation. In that way, centering Indigenous knowledge isn’t only the right thing to do from a social justice standpoint – it actively improves outcomes for society as a whole.

A key takeaway is that centering Indigenous knowledge systems transforms how we address climate emotions and mental health, and it does so in profoundly positive ways. It expands the definition of what counts as evidence or an “expert.” It insists that cultural safety and respect are part of well-being. It serves as a reminder that healing the land and healing the mind are connected.

This is not to position Indigenous Peoples as having all the answers or to say the challenges ahead aren’t significant. But it is to recognize that for too long, these knowledge systems were sidelined or actively oppressed, and that was a loss for everyone. Now, with the climate crisis upon us, there’s an urgency to learn from and uplift those systems. It requires humility – Western science and policy must be humble enough to realize they don’t have all the answers. It requires giving up some control and sharing power, which can be hard for institutions used to calling the shots. And it requires genuine relationship-building over the long term – showing up, listening, building trust, and following through on commitments.

The result of doing so is not just a more equitable world but a more resilient one. Picture climate initiatives that heal historical wounds while they fight climate change, that empower youth with a sense of identity while they learn job skills for the green economy, that protect a sacred grove of trees while also sequestering carbon. This is the kind of win-win outcome Indigenous frameworks strive for, because they don’t separate the well-being of people, other living beings, and the earth.

In conclusion, centering Indigenous knowledge systems is both a moral imperative and a practical strategy in the climate and mental health arena. It strengthens climate policy and practice by broadening what we consider valuable knowledge, by fostering cultural safer practices and trust, by supporting emotional healing alongside environmental action, and by restoring relationships with land and community. This is not only a matter of justice – acknowledging and righting the wrongs done to Indigenous Peoples – but it is also essential for our collective resilience in a climate-changed world. By walking forward with Two-Eyed Seeing, guided by both Indigenous wisdom and Western science, everyone both Indigenous and non-Indigenous Peoples stand a much better chance of not just surviving the climate crisis, but building a future where all of us, and the natural world we all exist in relationship to, can thrive.
