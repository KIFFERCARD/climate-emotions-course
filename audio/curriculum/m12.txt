Section 2: Distinguishing Emotions from Mental Health Conditions
Audio Overview — Transcript

When we talk about climate-related emotions in this course, we want to emphasize that feeling anxiety, grief, anger, or other emotions in response to environmental threats is normal, valid, and potentially adaptive — and does not necessarily indicate a clinical disorder. Emotions themselves are natural and typically short-lived reactions to what’s happening around or within us. They help us make decisions, stay motivated, and connect with others. In contrast, mental health conditions like those outlined in the DSM-5 or ICD-11 involve persistent patterns of thinking, feeling, and behaving that disrupt a person’s daily functioning over longer periods. While an emotion might pass on its own or be regulated by coping strategies, a formal diagnosis of a mental health problem involves symptoms that can linger for weeks, months, or even years, often without a clear situational trigger, and with an intensity or impact that far exceeds what we’d expect from emotional reactions. Understanding these distinctions helps clarify how normal emotional reactions, even those that feel intense or overwhelming, are not automatically indicative of a mental health disorder.

Take climate anxiety: it arises from worries about climate-related issues like rising sea levels, extreme weather events, and the overall state of the planet. Often it can spur constructive behaviours such as joining conservation efforts or advocating for policy change. Generalized Anxiety Disorder, on the other hand, reflects a chronic, uncontrollable worry that extends across multiple areas of life and persists for at least six months, interfering with everyday activities and often lacking a specific environmental trigger.

Similarly, if someone experiences trauma-like responses to a hurricane or wildfire—such as fear, grief, or shock in the immediate aftermath—that reaction can be adaptive and short-lived, serving as a natural alert system and a way of processing a frightening event. It becomes Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) only when those symptoms, such as flashbacks, nightmares, or hypervigilance, last well beyond the typical recovery window and disrupt a person’s ability to engage in everyday life and maintain well-being.

While it’s possible for persistent climate anxiety or recurrent extreme weather experiences to lead to anxiety disorders or PTSD, it’s important not to pathologize all eco-related distress. Climate grief, anger, and worry can be signs of healthy emotional engagement with very real challenges, rather than evidence of a psychiatric condition. Yet, when these emotions become overwhelming, unmanageable, or so consuming that they limit a person’s ability to function or find hope, we begin to see a crossover into clinical territory. This continuum also reminds us that personal identity, cultural context, and attachment to the land can magnify climate emotions, producing phenomena like solastalgia—a distress felt when one’s home environment is changing in ways that erode comfort and belonging. At the same time, collective action, open dialogue, and climate-aware mental health support can transform these difficult feelings into resilience and hope.

By acknowledging that climate emotions fall within a wide range of normal responses, and by recognizing the potential for them to escalate into mental health conditions if left unaddressed, we can validate people’s experiences while providing avenues for help. This includes making space in public health strategies, therapeutic settings, and policymaking for discussions about eco-emotions, without automatically labeling them as disorders. This approach helps ensure we remain sensitive to the genuine threat of climate change while offering support and skills that keep individuals and communities healthy, engaged, and empowered.
