Section 4: Climate Hope
Audio Overview — Transcript

Resilience reminds us that we can adapt to and even grow from adversity, but it doesn’t always tell us what keeps us looking forward—especially when we’re facing challenges as immense as those posed by climate change. That’s where hope enters the picture. While resilience helps us bounce back from hardships, hope moves us beyond mere survival to a vision of what might be possible in the future. In a world where headlines about extreme weather or ecological collapse can drive us into despair, hope acts as a lifeline: it provides the emotional and cognitive framework that sustains motivation, fuels collective effort, and helps us hold onto a sense of purpose even when the way forward seems unclear.

In this context, many thinkers have explored the power of hope to guide human behavior and imagination under conditions of profound uncertainty. Jonathan Lear’s notion of “radical hope,” for example, arose from the story of the Apsáalooke (Crow) Nation, who had to reinvent their cultural practices after everything familiar was taken from them. Radical hope means believing in a future good that may not yet be fully imaginable. Faced with climate upheaval, this mindset allows us to trust in humanity’s ability to adapt, innovate, and re-envision communal life, even when detailed solutions aren’t fully formed. Joanna Macy extends this perspective by describing “active hope,” a participatory stance that calls us to engage in mindful awareness, gratitude, and creative collaboration. Rather than waiting for something to spark optimism, active hope invites us to take part in shaping the future we want, thereby nurturing the resilience needed to keep going.

Another voice in this conversation is Rebecca Solnit, who underscores the vital role hope plays in uncertain times. She argues that when we acknowledge the ambiguity of the future, we create space for creativity and the potential for large-scale transformation. Climate change is perhaps the epitome of a global challenge that lacks tidy answers, yet Solnit’s work suggests hope can thrive precisely in that gap between what we know and what we can’t predict.

Christiana Figueres, a chief architect of the Paris Agreement, embraces a similar idea by championing “stubborn optimism,” which is neither naive nor dismissive of the gravity of the crisis. Instead, it demands a clear-eyed acceptance of facts alongside a determined conviction that solutions remain within reach. When people like Figueres or countless grassroots activists hold fast to this kind of optimism, they channel hope into tangible policy reforms and community-driven shifts toward sustainability.

Research and theory help illuminate why hope is so central to coping with climate emotions. In C. R. Snyder’s Hope Theory, the components of goal-setting, pathway thinking, and agency thinking come together to sustain momentum: once we articulate what we want to achieve, identify routes to get there, and believe in our ability to follow those routes, we’re far less likely to be immobilized by the scale of the problem. Positive psychology, advanced by scholars like Martin Seligman, shows how cultivating strengths such as gratitude and resilience can be transformative in a crisis. Meanwhile, theories like Bob Doppelt’s Transformational Resilience Framework and Barbara Fredrickson’s broaden-and-build approach both highlight that positive emotions—including hope—expand our problem-solving abilities and strengthen our social bonds, factors that are essential when communities must coordinate to address large-scale environmental threats.

What makes hope particularly relevant for climate emotions is that it can exist alongside grief, eco-anxiety, and fear, helping people remain engaged and connected despite uncertainty. Instead of removing distress, hope can reframe it as evidence of care and concern, supporting meaningful action and resilience. Psychologists like Renée Lertzman note that “environmental melancholia” often stems from feeling powerless or unable to express our ecological grief. But when hope is woven into our narratives—when we actively seek solutions, forge alliances, and celebrate small victories—despair becomes more manageable. Even a small group of neighbors implementing a composting program or a classroom planting pollinator gardens can kindle hope, reminding us that positive change is possible on multiple levels.

In practice, climate hope manifests in grassroots campaigns, where young people around the world gather to demand action, or in policy forums where global climate leaders push for ambitious climate targets. It surfaces in therapy sessions, where psychologists guide individuals from a place emotional  paralysis to one of agency, reassuring them that their deep concern is a sign of compassion, not futility. It also appears in educational initiatives that balance lessons on the severity of climate impacts with stories of successful conservation efforts and emerging green technologies. The result of this hope-driven engagement is a community of people who, while fully aware of the stakes, remain dedicated to envisioning and constructing pathways toward a sustainable future.

Ultimately, hope is not only about optimism or denying the challenges that lie ahead. It’s about affirming that meaningful change is possible, even when the journey seems long and the odds look daunting. It transforms our relationship to climate anxiety by giving us something tangible to work toward—a sense of shared purpose and the spark of possibility. In this way, hope doesn’t simply accompany resilience; it amplifies it. By focusing on collective imagination, meaningful collaboration, and the belief that our actions still matter, we tap into a source of strength that allows us to confront the climate crisis not just as a threat, but as an opportunity to reimagine how we live on this planet together.
