Section 2: Climate Emotions – Anger and Rage
Audio Overview — Transcript

Just as guilt and shame can rouse a desire for change—or, when left unaddressed, deepen despair—anger and rage are powerful emotional drivers that emerge when people confront the injustices and slow pace of progress tied to climate change.

Anger is often a response to perceived negligence or prioritizing profit over sustainability—whether by corporations, policymakers, or entire industries. This anger can ignite grassroots activism and hold leaders accountable, but it can also become overwhelming if channeled solely into confrontational or isolating behaviors.

Rage, a more intense and overwhelming version of anger, can arise when people feel truly powerless: they see storms growing more frequent, wildfires spreading unabated, and policy changes moving far too slowly. Left unmanaged, such rage can manifest in destructive behaviors or a numbing sense of futility. However, when people recognize these emotions and seek constructive avenues—such as community organizing, advocacy, mindful self-regulation, or sharing stories in supportive groups—they can turn anger into a catalyst for transformation.

For many Indigenous Peoples, emotions are often rooted in long-standing violations of land, sovereignty, and community well-being. Anger is not just about rising temperatures or failed climate policies; it is about how this connects to ongoing extractive practices, broken treaties, and systemic environmental violence that continue to threaten cultural survival. In contexts such as oilsands and pipeline development, Indigenous resistance has been driven by the threat of present and future environmental impacts on Indigenous lands and communities. Action and resistance has also resulted in extensive cultural and ecological protection, highlighting the power of collective action and Indigenous leadership in responding to these projects. In this way, climate anger can be a catalyst for leadership, organizing, and advocacy.

By validating these powerful emotions and providing outlets for them, individuals and communities can find the energy to push for significant policy changes, sustainable practices, and broader societal shifts, all while safeguarding their own mental health and resilience.
