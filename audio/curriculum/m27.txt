Section 3: Impacts and Inequities Related to Climate Change: Unpacking Marginalization and Vulnerability
Audio Overview — Transcript

Just as it is helpful to understand how social and environmental conditions influence emotions, and individuals respond differently across their lives, it is also important to recognize that broader systems, inequities, and social determinants of health shape individual and community experiences, exerting powerful influences on how we experience the world. In particular, structures and systems that cause marginalization and vulnerability are important to consider. These concepts are closely connected but distinct, and each reveals important truths about who bears the emotional and physical burden of climate change—and why.

Vulnerability refers to the degree to which someone is at risk from climate impacts. It is shaped by social conditions and structures, like accessibility of supports and resources. It can be influenced by physical characteristics, and how these characteristics interact with social structures. For instance, being very young or elderly, or living with a chronic illness may make it harder for a person to recover from extreme heat, hurricanes, or other environmental challenges. While vulnerability is often viewed as an inherent trait, it’s not fixed and social conditions are a key part of this dynamic. For example, an older adult living in a well-resourced neighborhood with access to healthcare, cooling centers, and transportation is less vulnerable to extreme heat than an older adult living in poverty without those supports. In this way, vulnerability is primarily about the systems that protect or neglect people’s well-being, and importantly, it can be reduced through equitable policies, fair resource distribution, and support.

Marginalization is more explicitly about social exclusion and the hierarchies that perpetuate it. Various systems of oppression, including but not limited to those based on gender, race, or colonial legacies, shape who is centered, what is prioritized, and who has access to power, resources, and decision-making. These hierarchies don’t just affect who experiences climate impacts—they shape whose emotions are validated and whose suffering is ignored. Indigenous communities, for example, often face unique grief and anger as the lands they’ve stewarded for generations are degraded or stolen, yet their voices are frequently sidelined in environmental policy discussions. Similarly, racism ensures that communities of color are disproportionately exposed to environmental hazards, from toxic waste sites to urban heat islands, while their calls for justice are too often dismissed or delayed.

The intersection of marginalization and vulnerability creates a compounding effect. Take the example of aging. While growing older comes with natural physical vulnerabilities, those vulnerabilities can be mitigated with resources or exacerbated by a lifetime of marginalization. For example, someone who has faced systemic racism may experience an earlier onset of chronic illnesses due to stress and inequitable access to healthcare, making them more physically vulnerable to climate impacts. Similarly, women in patriarchal societies are often relegated to caregiving roles with fewer resources and may be more likely to experience gender-based violence during climate-related events, leaving them more exposed to the emotional and physical tolls of climate crises.

Colonization adds another layer of complexity, shaping both the historical and present-day context of climate emotions. Due to colonial policies and violence, many Indigenous communities face not only environmental degradation but also the deep impacts on cultural practices and identities tied to the land. Colonization’s ongoing impacts and legacy is not just ecological—it’s deeply emotional, amplifying grief, anger, and a profound sense of injustice. At the same time, Indigenous Peoples often embody remarkable resilience, drawing on traditional knowledge and intergenerational strength to confront these challenges.

Acknowledging these dynamics is essential because it shows us that climate emotions aren’t just about individual experiences—they’re shaped by systems of power and inequality. When someone feels despair, anger, or even hope in the face of climate change, those emotions don’t exist in isolation. They’re informed by whether they’ve been included or excluded from decisions, whether they’ve been protected from or left exposed to climate impacts, and whether their identity has been honoured or devalued.

By understanding the interplay between marginalization, vulnerability, and the systems that cause and perpetuate them, we can begin to address these disparities in meaningful ways and work towards well-being, equity, and thriving, just possibilities for all. This includes dismantling the systems—including patriarchy, racism, and colonization—that perpetuate these inequities while also providing targeted support for those most impacted. Ultimately, this approach allows us to not only reduce suffering but also create a more just and inclusive response to the climate crisis that addresses its roots and centers the voices and needs of those who have long been ignored by larger systems, decision-making processes and colonial culture.
