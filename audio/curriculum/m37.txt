Section 4: Therapeutic Approaches - Skills-based Approaches
Audio Overview — Transcript

When the stress of climate change becomes overwhelming—when worries about the future consume our thoughts, and the distress of witnessing environmental degradation weighs heavily—practical strategies for managing difficult emotions can be a lifeline. While acknowledging and validating climate emotions is a critical first step, it’s equally important to have concrete skills to ground ourselves in the present, cultivate awareness, and regulate the intensity of our feelings. Below, we explore several therapeutic and skills-based approaches that anyone can learn. These methods draw from a range of psychological theories, including mindfulness frameworks, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), and somatic (body-centered) techniques. Their common goal is to help us navigate emotional turbulence, regain a sense of safety, and restore our capacity to think clearly and act meaningfully.

Grounding is often the simplest place to begin. When worries about melting ice caps or extreme weather events propel our minds into spirals of worst-case scenarios, grounding brings us back to the present moment. This might involve focusing on tangible sensory details, like noticing the pressure of our feet on the floor or the texture of the chair we’re sitting in. Some people find it helpful to count objects in the room, recite a calming phrase, or focus on the feeling of breath moving in and out of the body. These practices draw upon ideas from Polyvagal Theory, which highlights how engaging the senses can shift us out of a fight-or-flight state. By anchoring ourselves to the here and now, we interrupt ruminative or catastrophic thinking patterns, giving the body and mind a chance to settle and reset.

Presencing, closely related to grounding, involves a more deliberate effort to remain open and receptive to whatever is unfolding in the present. Unlike grounding, which often uses concrete anchors, presencing relies on sustained awareness of our internal experience—our body sensations, our emotional states, even our resistance or hesitation. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) captures this spirit by encouraging us to accept difficult emotions as they are, without trying to numb or run away from them. In the context of climate anxiety, presencing could mean recognizing that beneath our fear lies deep care for the Earth, and allowing that realization to foster both self-compassion and motivation to engage. By facing uncomfortable emotions safely, we gradually learn to be with them rather than avoid them, a process that can spark clarity and stronger emotional resilience.

Emotion regulation skills take many forms and are woven into numerous therapeutic models. In CBT, reappraisal techniques help us challenge unhelpful beliefs, such as “We’re doomed no matter what” or “Nothing I do can make a difference.” By re-evaluating the evidence, we realize there are still meaningful steps we can take—whether it’s reducing personal emissions, advocating for policy change, or organizing community action. Shifting perspective in this way can dial down emotional intensity, preventing us from falling into despair or cynicism. Other emotion regulation strategies might involve self-soothing activities like listening to music, taking a warm bath, or going for a walk in nature. In the face of climate-related stress, these small acts of care can replenish our energy and restore our capacity to cope.

Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), developed by Marsha Linehan, provides an especially robust set of tools for situations where emotions run high. In moments of acute climate distress—perhaps after seeing footage of a devastated coastline—DBT’s distress tolerance techniques can offer immediate relief. Holding an ice cube to focus the mind on physical sensation, using paced breathing to slow a racing heart, or practicing a brief “temperature change” method (such as splashing cold water on your face) can quickly bring the nervous system back from the edge. While DBT is often associated with treating intense emotional dysregulation, its core skills of mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness are broadly applicable for anyone grappling with strong feelings, including those triggered by the global climate crisis.

Somatic and embodiment practices highlight the physical dimension of our emotional lives. Whether through progressive muscle relaxation, yoga, or more specialized trauma-informed methods like Somatic Experiencing, the idea is to release tension trapped in the body and promote a deeper sense of safety. This can be especially helpful for those who feel immobilized by climate grief or eco-anxiety—emotions that can manifest as muscle tightness, shallow breathing, or persistent fatigue. By consciously moving, stretching, or even dancing, we can restore balance to our autonomic nervous system and regain a sense of vitality and agency.

Relational strategies are another key aspect of managing difficult emotions. Reaching out to friends, family, or support groups can amplify the benefits of any of these techniques. Talking about climate-related fears or frustrations isn’t always easy, but sharing the emotional weight can be a powerful antidote to isolation and hopelessness. In therapeutic models like Emotion-Focused Therapy, simply having someone validate our grief or anxiety can lessen the pain, fostering a sense of connection and mutual support. In turn, these interpersonal moments of understanding often galvanize us to take collective action, reminding us that we are not alone in our sense of urgency about the planet.

As this module introduces skills-based approaches to emotional regulation and climate resilience, Indigenous knowledge systems offer frameworks developed since time immemorial for cultivating these capacities through story, ceremony, land-based activities and deep attentiveness to place. Skills are not only taught, they are lived. The concept of Dadirri or deep listening, shared by Miriam Rose Ungunmerr-Baumann reflects this relational awareness through attentiveness to self, others, and the natural world, supporting balance and grounding.

All of these practices—grounding, presencing, emotion regulation skills, DBT techniques, somatic and embodiment work, and effective relational support—ultimately serve the same purpose: to guide us through the waves of emotion that accompany our growing awareness of climate change. They do so by influencing us on multiple levels: physiologically, by calming the body’s stress response; cognitively, by challenging destructive thought patterns; and socially, by building bridges with others who share our concerns. In this integrative way, we can develop the emotional flexibility necessary to face reality without succumbing to despair, and to remain active participants in creating a more hopeful and sustainable future.
