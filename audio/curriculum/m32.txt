Section 4: Types of Coping
Audio Overview — Transcript

Imagine you’ve just recognized your climate anxiety, acknowledging that it can sometimes push you toward positive action, or just as easily slide you into feeling stuck. Differentiating between more adaptive and less responses was our first step, but knowing that distinction is only half the story. The real question is: how can we channel our feelings in ways that preserve well-being and encourage meaningful  engagement with the challenges of a changing environment? That’s where the concept of “coping” comes in, offering us a framework for understanding how we manage stress, regulate our emotions, and maintain hope when facing threats as vast and complex as climate change. And as we explore these various ways of coping, we’ll also lay the groundwork for building the psychological resilience that will guide us in the modules ahead. Coping, at its core, is about how we respond when life demands more of us than usual. In the context of climate change, it might involve everything from trying to reduce our personal climate impacts to reminding ourselves that we are not alone in our fears. It is also important to recognize that understanding of coping and resilience are not universal. For example, some Indigenous perspectives may emphasize relational, land-based, and community-connected approaches that can expand how we think about these responses beyond individual psychological coping strategies. This broader framing can be held alongside the coping models that follow, which are primarily grounded in Western theory.

One of the most familiar types of coping is known as problem-focused coping. This involves taking direct action to address the source of stress—in the case of climate change, this might look like action to mitigate climate change such as deciding to improve your home’s insulation, organize a local beach cleanup, or lobby policymakers for more sustainable regulations. Psychologists like Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman highlighted that problem-focused strategies are particularly effective when a stressor is at least somewhat under our control. If you can identify tangible steps to mitigate the threat (or your contribution to it), you’re more likely to feel a sense of agency and competence. Of course, climate change is a global concern that no single person can solve in isolation, yet any concrete effort can still bring relief by transforming helplessness into purposeful engagement.

But not every stressor can be easily changed, which is where emotion-focused coping becomes another important resource. Instead of attempting to fix the external situation, we turn inward to regulate the emotions that arise. This could involve talking with friends who share our eco-anxiety, spending time outside in a green space such as a park or forest, practicing mindfulness during reports of extreme weather events, or engaging in grounding activities that restore our sense of balance. Thinkers like James Gross and John Bowlby have taught us that emotion-focused strategies can be particularly effective when a challenge (like global environmental shifts) feels inescapable or overwhelming. By learning to reappraise our distress—recognizing that fear is natural, but not letting it block us entirely—we help ourselves stay afloat emotionally. Without such self-care, the weight of climate news can accumulate to the point of burnout or even numbness, cutting us off from the motivation needed to remain actively involved in solutions.

Sometimes, however, the best approach is neither to solve the problem directly nor simply to soothe our feelings, but to search for meaning in adversity. This is where meaning-focused coping comes in, reflecting insights from existential psychology and positive psychology. When it feels as though no amount of personal recycling or petition-signing can adequately address threats on a planetary scale, we can still ask ourselves what values, connections, or long-term hopes might be nurtured in the midst of uncertainty. Perhaps this crisis draws us closer to our community or inspires us to develop new eco-friendly skills. Through conscious reflection, we can find a sense of purpose that fortifies our resolve to keep going—even if the broader landscape of climate issues remains daunting. Viktor Frankl argued that even in suffering, we can maintain a sense of agency by intentionally selecting the mindset we want to adopt and deriving meaning. Applied to climate change, that might mean reframing your activism as a moral calling or a way of honoring the environment for future generations.

On the other end of the spectrum lies avoidance coping, which serves its own purpose, but can be more destructive. When the magnitude of global temperatures rising or species disappearing feels too big to bear, the urge to look away can be strong. Avoidance can manifest as outright denial of scientific evidence, or it might take a subtler form: doomscrolling social media while feeling unable to act, or numbing the discomfort with distractions and habits that can impact well-being. Psychologists point out that avoiding distress is sometimes necessary in the very short term—particularly if you need to catch your breath after a barrage of dire headlines. Yet over the long run, avoidance keeps us from processing crucial information and from finding constructive outlets for our anxieties. Eventually, it can foster cynicism, hopelessness, and even deeper isolation. So while we might occasionally need a mental break from grim projections, relying on this strategy too heavily can cut us off from the understanding and collaboration required to address real environmental challenges.

These coping approaches aren’t rigid silos, nor do we need to choose just one. These strategies can be treated as different tools in a larger toolkit of resources and coping approaches. In fact, coping is often most effective when we adaptively blend approaches, shaped by our personal values, the nature of the stressor, and the support systems and resources available to us. In the face of climate change, for instance, you might launch a problem-focused plan—perhaps getting involved with local reforestation efforts—while at the same time engaging in emotion-focused self-care like regular therapy or meditation. If the problem feels too vast, finding meaning in collective efforts can sustain motivation. The key is flexibility, reflection, and recognition of what is needed — to recognize when a situation calls for strategic action and when it requires compassion for ourselves and others. By weaving these types of coping into our daily lives and relationships, we create a platform not only for preserving our mental health but also for making meaningful strides in the broader struggle for environmental well-being.

This understanding of coping lays a solid foundation for our next exploration: building psychological resilience. Whether we’re taking individual and collective climate action, forging community bonds, or simply learning how to soothe our climate anxieties in healthy ways, it’s all about creating a toolbox of strategies that can sustain us through the highs and lows of an uncertain future. Resilience does not mean never feeling distressed—it means having the capacity to adapt, even when the news is overwhelming or the obstacles feel insurmountable. In the next section, we’ll take these insights on coping a step further and discuss how to cultivate the kind of inner strength and communal support that enable us not just to survive in a changing world, but to thrive in it.
