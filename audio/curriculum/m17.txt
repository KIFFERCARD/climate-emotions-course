Section 2: Climate Emotions – Guilt and Shame
Audio Overview — Transcript

Building on the sense of loss and sadness that characterizes ecological grief and solastalgia, another facet of our emotional response to climate change involves guilt and shame—two feelings that can be both motivating and deeply distressing. Climate guilt arises when individuals believe their behaviors, or even their inaction, contribute to environmental harm, whether that’s through daily habits like consuming excessive single-use plastics or driving a high-emissions vehicle. Climate shame, meanwhile, takes shape when people feel fundamentally inadequate or unworthy because they see themselves (or humanity at large) as failing to meet the moral imperative of protecting the planet. A helpful way to distinguish between them is that guilt often takes the form of “I did something harmful” whereas shame centers on the belief “I am harmful”.

These emotions can spur meaningful efforts toward reducing one’s carbon footprint, advocating for greener policies, or supporting environmental organizations, as guilt or shame sometimes triggers a desire to take corrective action. However, when left unaddressed, they can also feed into cycles of despair, powerlessness, or avoidance, especially if the crisis feels insurmountable.

Guilt and shame can also carry cultural dimensions. Individualistic societies may emphasize personal accountability, heightening guilt about specific individual actions. By contrast, collectivist cultures might experience a more communal sense of shame tied to group responsibility for environmental harm.

While many Western discussions of guilt and shame may focus on individual responsibility for environmental harm, Indigenous worldviews often emphasize the importance of relational accountability and responsibility to the land, to ancestors, and to future generations.

Recognizing that guilt and shame are natural reactions to a threat of this magnitude is the first step; the next is transforming them into constructive engagement. Strategies might include seeking community support, talking with mental health professionals familiar with eco-anxiety or climate distress, or finding practical ways to align personal values with everyday choices—such as cutting back on waste, joining local climate initiatives, or helping shape broader systemic changes. By channeling guilt or shame into proactive measures, individuals can mitigate their own sense of helplessness and contribute to a collective effort that fosters hope and resilience in the face of ever-accelerating climate impacts.
