Section 2: Climate Emotions – Denial and Disengagement
Audio Overview — Transcript

Sometimes, the enormity of climate change can feel so overwhelming that our minds instinctively protect us through denial or disengagement. Denial involves outright rejecting or downplaying scientific evidence, often because accepting it would force us to confront uncomfortable truths about our lifestyle, priorities, or values. Cognitive dissonance plays a key role here: when new information clashes with deeply held beliefs or self-image, it can be easier—at least in the short term—to dismiss that evidence rather than rethink our actions or assumptions. For instance, a person who values convenience might deny the severity of climate change to avoid feeling guilty about day to day actions.

Disengagement, on the other hand, isn’t always an explicit rejection of the facts but rather a decision—conscious or not—to distance oneself emotionally from the topic. It might look like tuning out the news, sidestepping conversations, or shrugging off climate-related updates with a “what can I do?” attitude. This response often arises from a sense of powerlessness or fatigue. When people feel their individual efforts won’t make a dent in such a colossal crisis, it becomes tempting to cope by simply “checking out” of the issue.

While denial and disengagement may appear to be signs of ignorance or indifference, psychological research suggests they often serve as protective mechanisms that help people manage discomfort, anxiety, guilt or feelings of helplessness. Climate change is a complex and existential threat, and people respond to that threat in different ways depending on their experiences, beliefs, values and social environments. Both denial and disengagement can serve as psychological shields, offering temporary relief from the anxiety and responsibility that come with acknowledging climate threats.

By addressing the underlying fears—like losing a cherished way of life or confronting guilt over one’s carbon footprint—we stand a better chance of gently guiding individuals out of denial or disengagement. Building a sense of efficacy is also important. Small, achievable actions and participation in community efforts can help people move from feelings of powerlessness towards a greater sense of agency and hope. Through constructive dialogue, shared problem-solving, and community support, we can encourage more people to confront the climate crisis in proactive ways, harnessing human resilience and creativity instead of hiding behind emotional defenses.

Before concluding, it is also important to recognize that denial and disengagement are not only individual psychological responses, but also exist in broader historical and structural contexts. Historically, links between colonialism, environmental degradation, and climate change have often been minimized within institutional and policy settings. However, these connections are now increasingly being recognized in scientific and policy frameworks. By contrast, Indigenous Peoples have long recognized the connection between colonialism and climate change, observing and responding to a changing environment over generations. This perspective expands our understanding of denial and disengagement, highlighting political and historical contexts that shape how climate change is recognized, understood and acted upon.
