Section 4: Climate Justice
Audio Overview — Transcript

The previous modules have talked about the individual, systemic, peer, and community based strategies for promoting climate well-being and resilience. Across all these levels, one concept is critical to keep in mind: climate justice.

When we talk about climate justice, we’re talking about more than just tackling climate change. It’s about addressing the deep social, economic, and political inequities that determine who is most affected by the crisis and who has the most power to shape its solutions. At its heart, climate justice recognizes that this isn’t just an environmental issue—it’s a human rights issue. And the core question it asks is: how do we address the systems of inequity that drive these harms to people and planet, and ensure that those who have contributed the least to the problem are not the ones who bear the heaviest burden?

One way to think about this is through a few key approaches to justice. To start,  distributive justice focuses on the fair allocation of resources and responsibilities. For example, we know that wealthier nations have historically contributed the most to greenhouse gas emissions, yet it’s often low-income countries and marginalized communities that face the worst impacts—floods, droughts, rising sea levels. Distributive justice calls for those who’ve benefited most from climate-contributing processes and industrialization to step up and take action proportionate to their impacts, such as by funding adaptation projects, sharing technology, and cutting their own emissions more drastically.

Then there’s procedural justice, which is all about who gets a say in decision making. Imagine crafting policies about rebuilding after a devastating hurricane but not including the voices of the people whose homes were destroyed. Unfortunately, that’s a reality for many marginalized groups, whose perspectives are excluded from broader decision-making processes. Procedural justice demands inclusivity—it insists that solutions be co-created with those most affected, not imposed from above. It’s not just about having a seat at the table; it’s about having real influence on what’s decided.

Another key approach is restorative justice, which focuses on repairing harm. Think about the historical exploitation of land and resources in colonized regions—this wasn’t just environmental degradation, it was tied to the systemic oppression of people and cultures. Restorative justice asks: how do we make amends? This could mean reparations for communities displaced by climate disasters or investments in rebuilding ecosystems and cultural practices that have been eroded over time.

And then there’s intersectional justice, which is rooted in the understanding that people’s experiences of climate change are shaped by overlapping systems of oppression. For example, a disabled woman of color living in a low-income community faces challenges at the intersection of ableism, racism, and economic inequality, all of which make her more vulnerable to climate impacts due to these systems of oppression. Intersectional justice helps us see these complexities and interconnectedness of these issues and craft solutions that address them holistically. Disability justice further emphasizes accessibility, equity, and interdependence. Building on these, transformative and healing justice approaches go deeper by addressing root causes of harm and focusing on collective healing and systemic change.

These frameworks are not only theoretical, they are reflected in practice such as community-led initiatives. Across many contexts, Indigenous and local communities are leading climate solutions through land stewardship, cultural revitalization and community-owned renewable energy projects. These approaches combine ecological restoration with local well-being, self determination and economic opportunity.

Policy also plays a major role. Governments can implement climate justice through things like “just transition” policies that support workers in high-emission industries as economies shift toward sustainability. Or by funding international climate reparations—essentially paying back the debt wealthier nations owe to those who’ve been disproportionately affected by climate change.

Youth and grassroots movements also often embody climate justice in action. For example, movements where young people demand accountability not just for the planet but for social systems that perpetuate harm. These movements amplify marginalized voices and push for systemic changes, like divesting from fossil fuels or advocating for equitable climate education.

Climate justice is also deeply connected to mental health and emotional well-being. Climate emotions are often shaped by experiences of injustice. These are not individual problems but understandable responses to structural conditions. Climate justice can support wellbeing through several interconnected pathways.

But it’s not all easy. There are challenges, starting with power imbalances. Marginalized community voices are often dismissed, disregarded and disempowered, resulting in barriers and challenges to shaping policies or obtaining resources. There’s also the issue of tokenism, where groups are invited to the conversation but aren’t truly heard. And, of course, entrenched interests—whether it’s fossil fuel companies or industries reliant on unsustainable practices—actively resist change.

So how do we overcome these challenges? One way is by strengthening community capacity—providing resources, training, and funding to help local groups advocate for and implement climate justice solutions. Building alliances is another critical step—when grassroots organizations partner with governments, NGOs, and international bodies, they can amplify their voices and achieve greater impact. We also need to shift the narrative, educating people about climate justice, weaving a climate justice lens through current climate action, and framing it as a shared responsibility, not just an issue for those directly affected. And finally, we must hold systems accountable—through activism, advocacy, and even legal action, like lawsuits against polluting industries.

At its core, climate justice is about values: fairness, inclusion, and dignity. It reminds us that tackling the climate crisis isn’t just about reducing emissions—it’s about transforming systems of inequality and creating a future where everyone has the opportunity to thrive. Yes, it’s a monumental challenge, but by rooting our efforts in justice, we can build a world that’s not only more sustainable but also more equitable and deeply humane.
