Section 3: Developmental and Lifespan Perspectives
Audio Overview — Transcript

Just as social and environmental drivers create the broader context for our emotions, an individual’s developmental stage influences how they are impacted by, interpret and react to that context. Climate emotions aren’t static—they evolve as people move through different phases of life, shaped by their priorities, experiences, and abilities to engage with the challenges they face.

For children and adolescents, climate emotions often stem from growing awareness of the crisis combined with a limited sense of agency. Young people are at a stage of life where they are learning about the world and envisioning their future, which makes the existential nature of climate change particularly distressing. For some, this awareness might manifest as eco-anxiety and paralysis, fueled by fears about what their adult lives might look like on a warming planet. Others might channel their emotions into activism, inspired by peers and role models who frame action as a way to reclaim hope. For instance, First Nations, Métis and Inuit Youth are leading climate action, while incorporating traditional and cultural knowledge alongside contemporary approaches. The social environments of young people—family, schools, and media—play a crucial role here, shaping whether their emotions evolve into despair, empowerment, or somewhere in between.

In early adulthood, climate emotions often intersect with decisions about career, family, and lifestyle. This is a time when people are balancing idealism with pragmatism, grappling with questions about how to align their values with the demands of everyday life. For example, someone might feel guilt or shame over the carbon footprint of their commute or dietary choices, even as they struggle to make ends meet. Climate concerns during this stage are often entangled with questions of identity and purpose, making this a critical period for emotional and social support.

Midlife, for many, brings a shift in perspective as responsibilities expand to include families, careers, and communities. For parents, climate emotions can take on an added dimension of urgency, driven by concerns for their children’s future. The emotions here might be more complex, blending grief for what’s already been lost with anger or frustration at the slow pace of change. Yet, midlife can also be a time of great resilience, as people draw on their life experience and established social networks to take meaningful action, whether that’s joining local initiatives or advocating for policy change.

For older adults, climate emotions are shaped by a lifetime of experiences and a different sense of time. Some may feel grief or solastalgia as they witness firsthand the environmental changes that have unfolded over decades—landscapes they’ve known their whole lives disappearing, species they remember as children no longer present, and grief for lack of action and the futures of children and grandchildren. At the same time, older adults can often act as wisdom-keepers, offering historical perspective and grounding resilience for younger generations. In many Indigenous contexts, Elders support younger generations through storytelling, land-based teaching, and ceremony, helping them make sense of fear, anger, or grief related to environmental change. In some circumstances, older adults may also feel excluded from climate conversations or activism due to ageism or mobility challenges, underscoring the importance of intergenerational approaches that honor their contributions.

Across all stages of life, there is a shared need for connection and validation. Whether someone is a teenager frightened by their first news of melting ice caps or a retiree mourning the loss of a familiar coastline, their emotions are a functional response to the reality they face. By recognizing how these responses shift and evolve over the lifespan, we can provide targeted support that respects where people are in their journey. By integrating this developmental lens through interventions such as education, community engagement, or policies that address specific age-related concerns it can help us meet people where they are while fostering more meaningful engagement, strengthening resilience, and supporting adaptive responses to climate-related challenges across the lifespan.

In the next module, we’ll expand this perspective to consider how overlapping factors like marginalization and vulnerability further shape the emotional landscape of climate change, adding critical nuance to our understanding of who feels the burden most and why. Together, these insights deepen our capacity to respond with compassion and create solutions that are both inclusive and effective.
