Section 4: Multi-level Responses - The Role of Health and Mental Health Support Systems
Audio Overview — Transcript

As discussed throughout this course, climate emotions can't be tackled alone. The following three modules address the importance of health systems, peer support systems, and community-based approaches to support individuals in coping with climate change. This module focuses on health and mental health systems.

The first and perhaps most fundamental role of these systems is early, proactive support. When people encounter feelings of dread or grief over the changing environment, health professionals are often on the front lines—whether through a regular doctor’s visit, a community outreach program, or mental health hotlines. By providing psychoeducation, basic coping tools, and referrals to specialists when needed, these systems can prevent climate stress from escalating into chronic anxiety or depression. Early recognition and intervention also help break the stigma around climate emotions, sending a clear message that stress related to heatwaves, wildfires, and rising sea levels is not “overreacting” but rather a valid, human response that warrants professional attention.

Beyond the individual level, mental health and health systems have a responsibility to promote broader resilience. Populations who are disproportionately impacted, such as low-income families, Indigenous communities, or those living in disaster-prone areas often face compounded risks. Here, culturally responsive interventions are essential. Care might involve both trauma-informed therapy (acknowledging experiences of environmental displacement) and structural advocacy (addressing inequities like unsafe housing or lack of access to quality healthcare). The World Health Organization’s emphasis on climate-resilient health systems aligns with this goal, urging providers to integrate mental health into everything from primary care visits to disaster preparedness plans.

Community resilience further grows when mental health frameworks are woven into crisis response and recovery. For instance, psychological first aid (PFA) can be part of an emergency plan just as naturally as food and water distribution. By helping survivors deal with shock, grief, or fear, health professionals foster a sense of safety and hope at a critical moment. Longer-term, these same systems can guide support groups, therapy sessions, and collective healing rituals that restore a sense of connectedness within a traumatized community.

Equally important is the advocacy role that health professionals can play. Physicians and mental health specialists can highlight the psychological toll of climate instability at both the organizational and policy level, pushing for evidence-based responses that address both physical and emotional well-being. They can champion legislation for clean air or improved disaster response funding, knowing that robust environmental policies also lessen the mental health burden on the communities they serve. Integrating mental health perspectives into broader climate policy means not just treating the symptoms of eco-anxiety, but also working at the systemic level to reduce the root causes.

It is also essential that systems move beyond Western clinical models alone and actively create space for, culturally safe and supportive Indigenous-led approaches to healing which may offer relational and land-connected understandings of wellness in the context of climate-related mental health.
