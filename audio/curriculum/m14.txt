Section 2: Climate Emotions - Worry and Anxiety
Audio Overview — Transcript

When examining specific climate-related emotions, a starting point can be discussing worry and anxiety—two responses that have become increasingly common. In this module, we draw a distinction between climate anxiety and worry based on intensity. However, we also want to note that climate anxiety is often used to capture a wide range of experiences of climate distress. In research, media, and everyday conversations, the term is frequently used as a broad umbrella for emotional responses to climate change. Additionally, we do not use this to signify a clear cut off between the two, but rather reflect that these experiences may require different types of action and support to navigate.

Climate worry involves a pervasive sense of unease or concern about the planet’s health, and it often arises when people learn about climate impacts, such as melting glaciers, endangered species, intensifying storms, or the impacts of these on people. You might picture someone scrolling through news headlines about droughts, feeling a twinge of dread that won’t quite let go. This sense of worry can be unsettling, but it also serves a purpose: it alerts us that something important is at stake, motivating us to pay closer attention.

Climate anxiety, by contrast, represents a more intense, chronic form of distress. Instead of a passing worry, it can feel like a persistent fear of what the future holds. This anxiety can manifest in physical ways, such as sleepless nights or chronic tension, and can become mentally overwhelming, leading to a sense of powerlessness or even avoidance of climate news altogether. The difference between worry and anxiety often lies in how long those feelings linger and how deeply they interfere with daily life. While climate worry may spur you to sign a petition or switch to a more eco-friendly routine, climate anxiety can make it difficult to concentrate at work or find joy in once-cherished activities.

Climate worry and anxiety are having impacts in various stages of life. Younger generations, those who feel they’ll be living with the consequences the longest are particularly affected. For instance, high school and college students may feel paralyzed by the enormity of the crisis, especially if they perceive political leaders as unresponsive. At the same time, mid-career professionals might worry that they’re not doing enough to protect their children’s future. Older adults may worry about their well-being, the future of their grandchildren, and that they may not see necessary climate action within their lifetimes. By recognizing these reactions as legitimate, we can begin to shift away from dismissing climate-related concerns and move towards providing supportive outlets, like therapy groups focused on eco-distress or community forums that channel anxiety into meaningful action.

It’s important to note that climate worry and anxiety aren’t inherently “bad.” They can serve as vital signals, highlighting the urgency of climate change and galvanizing efforts toward sustainable solutions. However, when anxiety becomes pervasive—leading to chronic stress, persistent insomnia, or heightened isolation—it may signal the need for additional support. Mental health providers and community leaders are increasingly developing interventions tailored to climate concerns, such as normalizing discussions of eco-worries, teaching mindfulness techniques that help people cope with intrusive thoughts, or fostering social connections that counteract feelings of helplessness.

By distinguishing between worry and anxiety, we can better understand the emotional landscape people inhabit when facing environmental realities. Worry might prompt a practical first step, like installing solar panels or reducing plastic waste, whereas anxiety might hamper a person’s ability to even begin addressing these issues without professional help or collective support. Both emotions, however, reflect a deeply human response to genuine threats. In upcoming modules, we’ll explore additional climate emotions, from grief and guilt to anger and hope, aiming to provide a comprehensive view of how ecological disruptions affect our emotional lives and what we can do about it. For now, recognizing and validating worry and anxiety is an essential first step toward maintaining our mental well-being amid the challenges ahead.
