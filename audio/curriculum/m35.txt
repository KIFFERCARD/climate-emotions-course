Having explored the various ways people cope with climate-related stress—whether through problem-solving, emotion regulation, meaning-making, or, at times, avoidance—we are now ready to turn our attention to the concept of resilience. If coping is about the immediate strategies we use to manage stress, psychological resilience is the capacity to rebound, adapt, and sometimes even grow stronger in the face of adversity, trauma, or prolonged challenges. It is not a static trait that some people have and others lack; rather, resilience shifts over time, shaped by interactions between our biology, our psychological makeup, and our social environment. In the context of climate change, resilience becomes vital not only for preserving one’s mental health under sustained ecological threats but also for sustaining long-term engagement in solutions.

Building psychological resilience is essential in navigating the emotional challenges posed by climate change. Psychological resilience is the capacity to adapt and recover when faced with adversity, trauma, or significant stress. It’s about maintaining or regaining mental well-being despite challenges. Resilience is influenced by a mix of biological, psychological, and social factors and is not a static trait—it can be developed and strengthened over time.

Many theorists have studied resilience, each offering a different lens into how and why some individuals bounce back more readily than others. Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman’s cognitive appraisal theories, which emphasize how we perceive and interpret stress, also underpin our understanding of resilience. When we see a challenge (say, the possibility of rising sea levels affecting our town) as something we can address—through community planning, policy advocacy, or local volunteerism—we are more likely to respond with hope and motivation. Research in developmental psychopathology reinforces a similar principle by showing that certain risk factors—such as poverty, trauma, or chronic stress—can be mitigated by protective factors like emotional support, robust coping skills, or consistent mentorship. When positive influences outweigh adverse conditions, the likelihood of adapting successfully goes up. The same is true of effective coping methods: those who engage in problem-focused or emotion-focused techniques in a balanced way often fare better in the long run. This process can even lead to what Tedeschi and Calhoun describe as post-traumatic growth, in which adversity spurs a deeper sense of purpose, gratitude, or stronger interpersonal relationships.

Because resilience is so multifaceted, several frameworks have emerged to describe how we can intentionally foster it. The 7 Cs Model, for example, focuses on dimensions such as competence, confidence, and connection, underscoring that young people need genuine opportunities to develop skills, believe in their own abilities, form trusting bonds, and learn healthy coping. Likewise, the Resilience Framework developed by Hart and Blincow highlights “basics,” “belonging,” and “learning” as foundational layers—ensuring physical safety, nurturing relationships, and teaching problem-solving skills—all of which are especially relevant when grappling with chronic threats like climate change. Ann Masten famously called resilience “ordinary magic,” pointing out that it often emerges from everyday protective systems, including stable caregivers, supportive friends, and basic opportunities to succeed Other models add more explicit focus on meaning, optimism, and positive emotions. Martin Seligman’s PERMA model, for instance, suggests that Positive Emotions, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, and Achievement all feed into a person’s overall well-being and resilience. When applied to climate contexts, finding a sense of meaning or purpose in collective environmental efforts can sustain motivation even when progress feels slow. Similarly, Reivich and Shatté’s Three-P approach—reducing the tendency to personalize negative events, seeing them as neither permanent nor pervasive—helps individuals avoid the defeatist mindset that, for example, a single failed conservation project means nothing else can ever work.

Transitioning from these diverse frameworks, adopting a social ecological perspective offers a comprehensive approach to building resilience. The Social Ecology of Resilience Model emphasizes the interconnectedness of individual, interpersonal, and community strategies, recognizing that resilience is most effectively developed through multi-level support systems. In practical terms, building resilience can happen at multiple levels. On an individual scale, people can learn to reframe difficult situations through cognitive-behavioral techniques, hone emotional regulation skills through mindfulness, or become more adept problem-solvers by practicing step-by-step strategies for taking action. Interpersonally, close-knit social support networks and opportunities for altruism are crucial. Helping others, whether by joining local cleanups or supporting friends who share climate anxieties, not only bolsters communal ties but can also buffer one’s own distress. At the community level, resilience is strengthened by creating robust safety nets—everything from accessible mental health services to ensuring that neighborhoods have safe, climate-resilient infrastructure. Initiatives that encourage collective problem-solving can offer a sense of control and hope, diffusing the isolation that often accompanies large-scale environmental threats.

Indigenous perspectives offer a relational and land-based understanding of what it means to be resilient. Resilience is not an individual trait to be developed in isolation. It is something nourished through connection to the land, social networks, culture, and to community. These connections provide sources of strength, identity, and well-being that support individuals and communities in responding to climate change.

Putting all these perspectives together highlights why resilience is such a dynamic and holistic process. When we talk about adapting to climate change or coping with the emotional toll of ecological instability, we’re essentially talking about building resilience. Our internal beliefs and coping strategies, our close relationships, and even the policies and resources of our broader communities shape our ability to recover and persevere. By exploring and applying these theories and frameworks, individuals, groups, and entire societies can cultivate not just the will to endure, but the creativity and commitment to thrive under uncertain conditions.
