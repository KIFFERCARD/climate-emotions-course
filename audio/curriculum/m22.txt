Section 2: Assessing and Screening for Climate-related Mental Health Conditions

Finally, it’s worth taking a moment to discuss how we can identify and measure climate-related emotions—not just in formal research settings, but also in everyday interactions where people are grappling with climate anxiety, grief, or a sense of powerlessness.

Because this is still a developing field, many practitioners begin with standard mental health tools like the GAD-7 for anxiety or the PHQ-9 for depression, then add follow-up questions specifically about climate worries. At the same time, new scales have emerged that target eco-distress more directly—like the Climate Anxiety Scale and various solastalgia or environmental distress measures—though they’re still undergoing testing and refinement in different cultural and age groups.

Indigenous perspectives remind us that assessments must be grounded in cultural context and relational understanding. Effective and respectful assessment in Indigenous communities requires more than checklists or clinical tools. It involves acquiring knowledge and understanding of local cultural and community contexts, building relationships and relational accountability through listening with humility, acknowledging historical and ongoing harms, and including Elders and Knowledge Keepers who hold insight into emotional and spiritual well-being.

Quantitative approaches can be informative, especially for identifying when regular concern crosses into more disruptive or debilitating territory. However, it’s equally important to recognize that these scales aren’t the final word on someone’s emotional reality. Often, the most powerful way to understand and support people dealing with climate emotions is through genuine human connection.

Active listening strategies—like asking open-ended questions, reflecting back what you hear, and refraining from judgment—can create a safe space for individuals to share their fears, anger, or guilt without feeling dismissed. In many cases, simply knowing that someone is willing to listen and validate these concerns can be more impactful than a numbered score on a questionnaire. As climate psychology continues to evolve, it’s essential to balance the insights gained from systematic measurement with the empathetic presence that comes from one person truly hearing another.
