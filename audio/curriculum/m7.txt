Section 1: An Introduction to Well-being and Emotions
Audio Overview — Transcript

This module provides a broad introduction to the concept of well-being and situates emotions within a holistic understanding of health. Before we explore the specific emotions that arise in relation to climate change, it is important to step back and consider what we mean by health and well-being more broadly. As you will see, these are concepts that have evolved considerably over time and across cultures.

For much of modern Western medical history, health was understood narrowly as the absence of disease. This biomedical model, which dominated from the 19th century onward, focused on identifying and treating specific pathologies in the body, often to the exclusion of mental, emotional, social, cultural, and spiritual dimensions of human experience. While this approach produced enormous advances in treating infectious diseases and acute illness, it left significant gaps in how we understand what it means for a person or community to truly thrive.

A major turning point came in 1948, when the World Health Organization defined health as "a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity." This definition, while sometimes critiqued for its absolutism, was revolutionary in recognizing that health encompasses far more than bodily function. Over the following decades, models of health continued to expand. The biopsychosocial model, articulated by George Engel in 1977, argued that biological, psychological, and social factors are all essential to understanding health and illness. More recently, frameworks like the social-ecological model have drawn attention to how environments, institutions, and policies shape health outcomes at the population level.

Yet even these expanded models have been critiqued for remaining rooted in Western paradigms that tend to separate mind from body, individual from community, and human beings from the natural world. Indigenous Peoples around the world have long held more integrated understandings of health and well-being, understandings that predate and often challenge the assumptions of Western biomedical and psychological frameworks. Many Indigenous knowledge systems conceptualize health as a state of balance among physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual dimensions, all of which are inseparable from relationships with land, community, ancestors, and the living world.

For example, the Medicine Wheel, used by many First Nations in North America, represents a holistic framework in which physical, emotional, mental, and spiritual health are interconnected and must be in balance. The Maori concept of Te Whare Tapa Wha in Aotearoa New Zealand similarly describes well-being as a four-sided house encompassing physical health, mental health, family and social health, and spiritual health, where weakness in any one wall compromises the whole structure. In many Aboriginal Australian traditions, health is understood through connection to Country, meaning that the well-being of people and the well-being of the land are inseparable. These are not abstract philosophies; they represent lived, practiced systems of knowing that have sustained communities for millennia.

This broader context matters enormously for how we approach emotions in relation to climate change. In this course, we deliberately focus on emotions rather than diagnosable mental health conditions. This is an intentional choice, not to dismiss the reality of clinical mental health impacts, but to recognize that the emotional responses people have to climate change are, in most cases, rational and proportionate responses to a genuine threat. Grief over the loss of a beloved landscape, anxiety about an uncertain future, anger at perceived inaction: these are not symptoms of pathology. They are expressions of care, connection, and moral engagement with the world.

By framing this course around emotions rather than diagnoses, we follow the lead of researchers and practitioners who caution against pathologizing what are fundamentally healthy human responses to extraordinary circumstances. This approach also aligns more closely with Indigenous and holistic understandings of well-being, which tend to view emotions as meaningful signals about the state of our relationships, rather than as problems to be managed or eliminated.

As we move through Section 2, you will explore theories of emotion, learn to categorize and distinguish different emotional experiences, and encounter the specific climate emotions that researchers have identified. Throughout, we encourage you to hold onto this holistic framing: emotions are not separate from our physical, social, and spiritual lives, and attending to them is an essential part of maintaining well-being in a changing climate.
