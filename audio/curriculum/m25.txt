Section 3: Social and Environmental Drivers
Audio Overview — Transcript

When talking about the social and environmental drivers of climate emotions, we’re really exploring the underlying forces that shape how people respond to the climate crisis emotionally. These drivers act as both the context and the catalysts for our feelings, whether it’s fear, grief, anger, hope, or some combination of emotions. By understanding these forces, we can better appreciate why people feel the way they do and how those emotions are a functional response to what’s happening around them.

On the environmental side, the drivers are often visible and tangible. Rising sea levels, extreme weather events, deforestation, biodiversity loss, and other climate impacts are not abstract ideas; they’re real and happening all around us. When a community is flooded or a wildfire devastates a beloved forest, it’s natural for people to feel fear, sadness, and even rage. These emotions are signals, alerting us to the fact that something is wrong and prompting us to take action to protect what we care about. Even subtle environmental changes, like shifts in seasonal patterns or the disappearance of familiar species, can trigger a sense of unease or grief, especially for those who feel deeply connected to the natural world. These environmental changes do not happen in a vacuum, shaped by systems of extraction and inequity, they are not neutral or inevitable.

Similarly, the social drivers of climate emotions are rooted in a variety of systems and relationships. As explored in previous sections, there are also a number of structural dynamics and inequities that contribute to disproportionate impacts and experiences of climate events and emotions.

First, there’s the role of political and economic systems. When people observe governments or corporations prioritizing short-term profits over long-term sustainability, it can lead to feelings of anger, betrayal, or powerlessness. Conversely, when leaders take bold climate action, it can foster a sense of hope and collective empowerment.

Additionally, social norms and cultural narratives can influence how we feel about climate change. For example, in some communities, discussing environmental issues is encouraged and celebrated, while in others, it may be dismissed or even stigmatized, leaving individuals feeling isolated in their concerns.

Media coverage can also play a role in shaping climate emotions. Constant exposure to catastrophic headlines can amplify feelings of anxiety or hopelessness, especially if the coverage lacks a focus on solutions. On the other hand, the media can also inspire hope and action when it highlights success stories, innovations, or community resilience in the face of environmental challenges.

It’s also important to consider how these social and environmental drivers interact. For instance, extreme weather events don’t just destroy infrastructure; they disrupt social networks, separate families, and strain community resources. This compounds the emotional toll, as people not only face the immediate loss of homes or livelihoods but also lose the support systems that help them cope. Similarly, systemic inequities and climate injustice mean that some communities, often those that contribute the least to climate change, bear the brunt of climate impacts while having the least resources to adapt, exacerbating feelings of vulnerability and injustice.

Building on the interaction between environmental and social drivers, for many Indigenous communities, environmental degradation cannot be separated from the impacts of colonization. Environmental degradation and colonial practices such as the destruction of ecosystems, displacement from traditional territories, the residential school system, restrictions on land-based practices, and resource extraction are not isolated events—they are also social and cultural traumas. These disruptions affect not only physical survival but also influence mental, spiritual, and community health. This helps explain climate emotions in the context of environmental change but also the broader systems and histories that shape those changes.

These drivers are interconnected, and their effects ripple across individual, community, and societal levels. But within this complexity lies an opportunity: by addressing the root causes of climate emotions, whether it’s addressing climate injustice, advocating for stronger environmental policies, amplifying hopeful narratives, or rebuilding social connections, we can help channel these emotions into constructive action. Climate emotions are not just signs of distress; they’re signals of what we value and love, and a call to protect it. By understanding the forces that shape these feelings, we can begin to respond in ways that build resilience, foster connection, and inspire meaningful change.
