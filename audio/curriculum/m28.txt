Section 3: Intersectionality
Audio Overview — Transcript

Building on the interconnected ideas of marginalization and vulnerability, we now turn to the concept of intersectionality—a framework that helps us understand how multiple aspects of a person’s identity and social position combine to shape their experiences, including their emotional responses to climate change. Intersectionality, a term coined by Kimberlé Crenshaw, highlights how systems of oppression like racism, sexism, ableism, and classism can overlap and compound, creating unique forms of discrimination and disadvantage. In the context of climate change, this framework allows us to see how these overlapping systems affect who bears the brunt of environmental harm and who is most impacted emotionally.

For example, an Indigenous woman living in a low-income coastal community faces intersecting challenges. She may contend with historical and ongoing legacies of colonization that have appropriated land and resources, banned ceremony and resilience strategies from her community, the gendered expectations of caregiving in the wake of climate disasters, and the economic barriers that limit her ability to relocate or rebuild. Each of these identities—Indigenous, female, and low-income—shapes her vulnerability, but it’s their intersection that creates a uniquely compounded burden. Similarly, a Black man living with a disability who resides in a flood prone area and faces mobility challenges, may experience racial discrimination, and systemic neglect which create barriers in accessing emergency support and evacuation services. This situation increases risk of harm or injury from extreme events and contributes to emotional distress.

Intersectionality is a particularly relevant lens when we consider climate-related mental health. Emotional responses like eco-anxiety, grief, and hopelessness don’t occur in isolation; they are shaped by the systems people navigate daily. For someone at the intersection of multiple marginalized identities, these emotions may be amplified by the added stress of systemic oppression, a lack of access to mental health resources, and a history of being excluded from environmental decision-making. At the same time, intersectionality encourages us to consider privilege and ways that systems can create advantages for some groups while creating barriers for others.

Additionally, as explored in the previous module, systems of oppression also make it more likely for marginalized people to experience climate-related disasters, evacuations, and harms. This can lead to deeper feelings of anger, despair, fatigue, hopelessness, or disconnection—but also to profound resilience, as individuals and communities draw on rich cultural traditions and collective strength to face these challenges.

Intersectional climate justice is a way forward, addressing not just the environmental aspects of climate change but also the social inequities that compound its effects. By centering the voices and needs of those most affected, intersectional approaches take steps towards creating solutions that are inclusive, equitable, and sustainable. For mental health, this means recognizing that people’s emotional well-being is tied to their varying degrees of access to resources, representation, and respect. It’s not enough to provide generic mental health support; we must create culturally informed, community-based interventions that honor the unique experiences of those at the margins.

For example, ensuring that climate-affected communities have access to trauma-informed mental health care, led by practitioners who understand the cultural and historical contexts of their struggles, is a step toward intersectional justice. At the same time, integrating mental health into broader climate policies—like funding green infrastructure in underserved neighborhoods or supporting land stewardship programs led by Indigenous communities—can address both the physical and emotional impacts of climate change. In this sense, an intersectional approach acknowledges that addressing and dismantling systems of oppression and supporting new and remembered ways of being that center values of justice, equity, respect, and care is a core part of climate and health work.

Ultimately, intersectionality reminds us that climate justice and mental health are inseparable. By embracing this framework, we move beyond one-size-fits-all solutions and toward a world where everyone has the opportunity to thrive—physically, emotionally, and environmentally. It’s a reminder that the fight against climate change is not just about saving the planet; it’s about creating a future that is inclusive, just, and caring.
